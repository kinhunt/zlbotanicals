# Research architecture source review

Checked 2026-09-09. Suggested trial and procurement workflows are editorial questions, not reported experiments.

## Green tea science: catechins, EGCG and material identity

Read green tea as a material description, not a single chemical. The EFSA opinion distinguishes green tea leaves, infusions, reconstituted drinks and concentrated supplement extracts, and notes substantial compositional variability. Its assessment uses EGCG content to compare otherwise different preparations.[1]

For an educational comparison sheet, keep total polyphenols, total catechins and EGCG in separate fields. Ask for the named analytes, method, reference standards, reporting basis, caffeine result and carrier declaration. EFSA specifically recommends labels include catechins and the proportion of EGCG; this recommendation is not our released specification.[1]

EFSA notes uncertainty in estimating actual EGCG exposure from dry-leaf content because concentrations change during preparation and storage. Development question: what survives the proposed extraction, dilution, heat cycle and storage period in the actual matrix? Record sampling time and assay basis before comparing retention. Do not infer a commercial extraction recipe from this opinion.[1]

Proposed workflow: define the intended serving and consumption occasion, obtain caffeine and catechin data, then agree acceptance criteria for taste, appearance and analytical stability with the formulator. Compare matched samples at the proposed pH, water composition and heat history. These are study-design questions, not completed ZL tests, a validated dose or a shelf-life promise.

Evidence type: EFSA scientific safety opinion synthesizing literature, human interventions and toxicological data, not a trial of a ZL product. The opinion discusses rat-hepatocyte experiments separately from human exposure. A cell result cannot establish a beverage benefit; a concentrated extract cannot inherit an infusion’s context simply because both are called green tea. No serving-dose recommendation is made here.[1]

## Centella asiatica science: triterpenes and topical evidence

The reviewed paper identifies asiaticoside, madecassoside, asiatic acid and madecassic acid as compounds of interest in Centella asiatica. Its abstract also discusses hydrogels, nanostructures and microneedles. A plant extract, an individual compound and a delivery system are different experimental materials, even when a title uses the same botanical name.[5]

For a comparison, ask which triterpenes are included in the reported total and how each is quantified. Keep botanical identity, plant part, extraction solvent, marker profile, carrier and microbiological requirements separate. A single total does not document the composition of a study material. These are requested fields, not analytical results for a stocked grade.

A microneedle paper cited in the review concerns dermal delivery of Centella asiatica, not ordinary bulk-powder dissolution. When reading a formulation paper, record whether a result belongs to the extract, a carrier system or their combination. Ask the supplier separately about extraction and fractionation; do not assume the reviewed delivery technology is present in a commercial ingredient.[2]

For a cosmetic development brief, define a topical format, intended market and permissible claims for qualified review; ask for compatibility, preservation and safety work specific to the final formula. An oral beverage is a separate assessment, not a translation of a topical project. The site does not offer a wound-treatment formula or certify that an ingredient is permitted in a destination market.

Evidence type: a 2024 literature review, not one controlled trial. Retrieved sections describe rat dermal-wound work, while the abstract discusses collected clinical trials and delivery systems. We checked the abstract and selected full-text passages, not every underlying protocol or risk of bias. Do not apply a rat-wound result or a specialized topical delivery outcome to a drink, normal skincare claim or untested ZL powder.[2][5]

## Monk fruit science: mogrosides, extract identity and sweetness

FDA’s response to GRN 000706 describes the notifier’s material as a mixture of compounds extracted from Siraitia grosvenorii fruit. The letter concerns specified sweetener uses and explicitly records exclusions for infant formula and foods under USDA jurisdiction. It does not describe a ZL material.[3]

For procurement comparison, distinguish a mogroside V result from a total-mogroside result, and request the chromatographic method and reporting basis. Record carriers, moisture and any other ingredients in a blend. Do not infer the total composition or a finished drink’s sugar status from one marker value. No purity, calorie or sweetness guarantee is established here.

Development questions: is the proposed material a fruit extract or a formulated blend; which extraction, purification and drying stages are disclosed; and what changed between samples? Request evidence of composition before and after a process change. A published review of fruit chemistry and applications is a map for questions, not a validated production procedure.[5]

Proposed sensory plan: compare coded samples against the chosen benchmark in the actual beverage or food matrix; record sweetness onset, persistence, off-notes and interactions with acids or flavors. Use the same serving temperature and blinded assessment protocol. Confirm labeling and destination-market suitability separately. This plan is a suggested review workflow, not an assertion that trials or a commercial formula already exist.

Evidence types: an FDA response letter about a notifier’s GRAS conclusion, and a 2024 review of fruit chemistry and applications. Neither is a clinical trial of this supplier’s sweetener. Exposure estimates described in the letter are not serving-dose recommendations. The review’s broad health language is not adopted as a product claim; no glucose-control, weight-loss or disease benefit is inferred.[3][5]

WITS distinguishes CIF imports from FOB exports, notes classification differences and recommends consistent reporters for mirror-data time series.[4]
