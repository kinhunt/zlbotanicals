# Choosing Paprika Extract: Oil-Based Colour or Water-Dispersible Preparation?

**Review draft — not published.** For food formulators and ingredient buyers. This is material-selection guidance, not an offer of a confirmed grade or a finished-product formulation.

A request for “paprika extract” leaves two important decisions open: are you buying red colour or chilli heat, and does the ingredient need to enter an oil phase or disperse through an aqueous recipe? The JECFA technical assessment distinguishes low-pungency colouring extracts from pungent capsicum oleoresins, and describes both oil-standardized extracts and water-dispersible preparations made with food emulsifiers.[1]

For an oil-based seasoning or coloured fat phase, start the sourcing discussion with an oil-standardized colouring extract. For an aqueous sauce, ask about a water-dispersible preparation and its complete composition. For a spicy seasoning, state the pungency requirement separately rather than expecting a red colouring extract to supply it. These are starting points for selecting samples, not confirmed performance in your recipe.

## The material behind the name

The 2014 JECFA specification defines paprika extract as a solvent extract of dried ground fruit pods of *Capsicum annuum*. It identifies capsanthin as the major colouring compound and describes the remainder of the extract as lipidic, varying with extraction solvent; commercial preparations may be standardized with refined vegetable oil.[2]

That is why a paprika extract should not be specified as though it were isolated capsanthin. The same specification describes a dark-red viscous liquid that is practically insoluble in water.[2] A supplier's “water-dispersible paprika” therefore needs its own composition and application description; the technical assessment explains that emulsifiers can be added to produce water-dispersible formulations.[1] Do not treat that description as proof of a transparent, molecularly dissolved colour.

| Obtain both red colour and chilli heat | A pungent capsicum oleoresin route, with colour and pungency specified independently | The assessment treats pungent oleoresin and paprika colouring extract as different product types.[1] | Separate targets for colour and pungency; do not approve one from the other |
| Dose colour through a dry seasoning premix | Ask whether a suitable dry formulated preparation can be sourced | A dry delivery request is a separate project; the principal specification reviewed here describes a viscous liquid.[2] | Actual carrier and colour loading, handling, blend distribution and how the premix is ultimately used; no dry grade is confirmed here |

An aqueous application does not automatically require a new extract chemistry: the commercial distinction may be the delivery system around the extract. Conversely, buying the same botanical name does not establish that an oil preparation and an emulsifier-containing preparation are interchangeable.

## Keep colour strength, carotenoid composition and pungency separate

The JECFA specification lists total carotenoids at not less than 7%, while capsanthin is not less than 30% **of total carotenoids**.[2] These are reference-specification statements, not ZL specifications, current stock values or certificates of analysis. The second percentage is not 30% capsanthin in the supplied preparation. These thresholds describe the extract in the cited reference specification; do not apply them automatically to a diluted or formulated preparation without identifying the material and reporting basis.

The source also assigns different analytical procedures: spectrophotometry for total carotenoids and HPLC for capsanthin.[2] Ask a supplier to identify the analyte, method, unit and reporting basis for every quoted value. Do not silently substitute a colour-strength number for a carotenoid mass percentage, or a pigment-composition percentage for the strength of the full commercial preparation.

Finally, retain capsaicinoids as a separate line. The reference specification gives them a separate limit and test.[2] A redder sample is not an analytical report of pungency. If a supplier offers a “de-flavoured” preparation, request the process description and sample for sensory evaluation: the technical assessment describes further processing to reduce flavour and aroma, but this does not establish that every offered grade is flavour-free.[1]

## What a useful sample comparison should decide

First compare preparations at a defined colour target in the actual food, rather than at equal raw-material addition alone. Record how much supplied ingredient each needs. If the objective is instead an equal-pigment comparison, use compatible assays and reporting bases and label that as a different experiment. Neither design proves which material has the lowest total use cost until real prices and application results are available.

For an oil-phase candidate, agree where it will be introduced and judge distribution and flavour after your real process. For a water-dispersible candidate, follow the supplier's dispersion instructions and inspect the actual sauce or dressing after processing and storage. Set the acceptable colour, uniformity, visible separation and flavour criteria before the comparison. Do not borrow a shelf-life claim, heat tolerance or recommended dose from a different recipe.

For a dry premix request, first establish what material is actually offered. A specification for the underlying colour extract does not by itself describe the carrier system, supplied-powder colour loading or mixing behaviour of a formulated powder. Keep this as an open sourcing request until documentation and samples identify the preparation.

## Start a sourcing discussion with a defined material, not just a plant name

A useful enquiry might read: “Paprika-derived red colour for an aqueous sauce; please propose a water-dispersible preparation, identify all carriers and emulsifiers, report colour strength with its method and basis, and provide the capsaicinoid specification. We will assess colour, flavour and dispersion through our process before approving a grade.” This is an example brief, not a real order or a claim that a matching preparation is in stock.

Include the destination market, food category, process conditions, packaging, required quantity and ingredient restrictions. ZL can discuss these requirements for sourcing and quotation; the specific grade, sample availability, documentation and suitability remain subject to project confirmation.

## Scope and regulatory boundary

This guide uses an official 2008 technical assessment for material-format descriptions and the later 2014 JECFA specification for identity and analytical distinctions.[1][2] The latter supersedes the tentative 2008 specification; the old assessment's unfinished evaluation must not be presented as the current safety position.[2] Neither document is a universal authorization for every food category or country. Confirm applicable current permissions, labelling and limits with the responsible regulatory team before use. No use level, shelf life, certification or batch performance is promised here.
