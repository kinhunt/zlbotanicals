# Opportunity card — material identity versus the endpoint that wins

Status: research-only EN/ZH comparison candidate; not integrated, independently edited or published. Discovery date: 2026-09-21 UTC.

## Hypothesis and trigger

A food buyer choosing between rosemary antioxidant extract, a water-soluble botanical brief and essential oil needs to distinguish material identity from success on a particular test. A retrieved primary study supplied a useful counterexample: essential oil had the lowest TBARS, but extract had the best overall sensory assessment in that pork system.[2] A second primary study showed higher peroxide value in the high-extract treatment alongside lower MDA than control in salmon pâté.[1] These are research triggers, not measured search demand.

Reader: food ingredient procurement and formulation teams. Commercial connection: frame a grade/sample enquiry around the intended material, addition process, sensory role and verification endpoint. No own stock, certification or supply claim.

## Alternatives explored and decisions

1. **Generic delivery-format buying guide — do not create another one.** Existing `2026-09-21-commercial-discovery/rosemary-food-antioxidant/selection.en.md` already covers oil-soluble/water-dispersible/dry formats, carnosic-acid assay and fat-filled milk-powder evidence. Repeating that structure adds little.
2. **Universal ranking of oil-soluble, water-soluble and essential-oil materials — rejected.** Retrieved studies do not compare all three with characterised matched doses. The pork “extract” cannot be identified as a water-soluble grade from its reported methods.[2]
3. **Essential oil is unsuitable for oxidation control — rejected by counterexample.** It had the lowest TBARS in the pork experiment; a categorical dismissal would contradict retrieved evidence.[2]
4. **Best antioxidant means best flavour — rejected.** The pork results separate these outcomes; its six-person panel is still too small and matrix-specific for universal acceptance claims.[2]
5. **One number proves shelf-life extension — rejected.** Different oxidation endpoints moved differently in the salmon experiment.[1] This package establishes neither microbial protection nor retail shelf life.
6. **E392 legal-dose calculator or current authorisation guide — deferred.** Institutional full text was recovered, but current consolidated EU annexes were not. A historical assessment is adequate for explaining the difference between composition specifications and use permissions, not for issuing today's food-category limit.[7]
7. **Selected output:** a material comparison with a focused account of oxidation-versus-sensory selection. Not another blank worksheet or diagnostic article. Keep useful method recommendations within the comparison.

## Evidence gained and limitations

- Two actual primary-study XML bodies, including methods/results, and one institutional EFSA full-text XML body recovered and archived.[1][2][7]
- Water-extraction stream described in the salmon paper, but only the lipophilic material tested there; no water-solubility measurements, turbidity trial or water-extract efficacy ranking retrieved.[1]
- Study dose is not proposed dose. Equal extract mass is not demonstrated equal active constituent mass.[2]
- Salmon abstract MDA unit conflicts with methods/table; nominal high-dose wording and measured constituent information also require caution. Absolute MDA figures and legal-dose calculations deliberately omitted.[1]
- E392's 90% denominator is total phenolic diterpenes, not total commercial ingredient; this was checked against the EU column, not the adjacent JECFA column, in EFSA Table 1.[7]
- No BrowserMan, community observation, social action, product purchase, GSC data, search-volume claim, sales statistic or own experiment. Search outputs used only to find full bodies.

Priority: useful evidence-ready comparison for parent review; not an automatic new-page priority. Evergreen question with a regulatory refresh requirement.
