# Tiger nut ingredients for fermented drinks: choose the base before choosing the culture

A fermented tiger nut drink is a useful development direction—but buying a bag of tiger nut flour and selecting a yogurt starter are not enough to define the product. The first decision is what actually enters the fermentation vessel: a pressed and screened beverage, a dispersion of whole flour, or a formulation made with defatted flour.

A 2025 study provides an unusually useful starting point. It compared four commercial lactic-acid-bacteria starters in tiger nut, carob and rice beverages, then examined composition, aroma and survival after simulated digestion. The tiger nut base was made by chopping, crushing, pressing and screening a water–tiger nut mixture; it was not simply flour stirred into water.[1] Commercial suppliers, meanwhile, offer materially different tiger nut ingredients, including extra-fine, traditional and defatted flours.[5][6]

The practical opportunity is to develop a fermented botanical base with a defined flavour and composition. It is not a shortcut to a low-sugar, high-protein or clinically proven probiotic claim. The discussion below uses third-party research and supplier literature—not ZL production trials, original ecommerce sampling or verified customer results.

## Start with the material, not the name

In the study, the starting recipe was 80% water and 20% tiger nut (*Cyperus esculentus*), followed by extraction and screening. The authors report an initial 5.5 °Brix for the resulting unfermented beverage.[1] The 20% input therefore should not be written into a finished-drink specification as 20% retained tiger nut solids. Neither does °Brix by itself identify how much glucose is available to a particular culture.

For sourcing, three routes deserve different conversations:

| Candidate route | What the available evidence actually describes | What to resolve before comparing cultures |
|---|---|---|
| Pressed and screened tiger nut base | The material used in the fermentation paper; a processed beverage rather than a whole-flour suspension.[1] | Extraction yield, retained solids, added ingredients and the exact heat history of the delivered base. |
| Extra-fine tiger nut flour | The supplier describes a milling route and customised technical specifications.[5] | Particle-size distribution, dispersion, sediment, and whether the customer will retain or remove insoluble material. “Fine” does not establish dissolution or fermentation performance. |
| Defatted tiger nut flour | The supplier's catalogue lists 9.1 g fat and 25 g fibre per 100 g for this named material.[6] | Whether a lower-fat, fibre-containing ingredient fits the intended drink, and how much of that composition remains after processing. These catalogue figures are not batch results or finished-drink nutrition. |

The defatted grade is not fat-free. Its catalogue also says “May contain traces of allergens,” without identifying which allergens in that passage.[6] That is a reason to request a current allergen statement, not to infer either a specific contaminant or universal allergen freedom.

The extra-fine product page says the supplier is working on variations, including a sterilised version.[5] This does not establish that every extra-fine grade is supplied sterilised. A purchasing specification should identify the actual delivered treatment rather than inherit a capability mentioned elsewhere on a website.

## What the fermentation experiment did—and did not—optimise

The researchers heat-treated the beverages at 85 °C for five minutes. After receipt at the laboratory, the tiger nut beverage needed an additional treatment at 80 °C for ten minutes because of its microbial load; the authors then reported no viable colonies detected.[1] This is a description of their samples and assay, not a validated thermal process for another ingredient or a claim of commercial sterility.

Fermentation took place in flasks containing 150 mL of beverage. The researchers compared 30 and 37 °C and subsequently tested three glucose-addition levels at 37 °C. For tiger nut, these were no added glucose, 7.5 g/100 mL and 15 g/100 mL.[1] These are experimental additions, not recommended factory doses or the measured residual sugar in the finished drink.

The authors selected 37 °C and the intermediate glucose level for subsequent work, considering acidification and microbial counts. However, their broad statement that this level gave the best counts does not hold for every tiger nut starter in the original table.[1]

All three glucose-level columns were tested at 37 °C. The zero ± term is reproduced as published, not interpreted as proof of zero biological variability.[1]

For VEGE061, the intermediate addition produced a lower reported mean count than either of the other two levels. For VEGE022 and VEGE053, the highest reported mean was at the higher addition. This is not a statistical ranking: the table does not establish significance for these within-row contrasts.[1] It does show why an “optimal fermentation” condition should not be sold as the highest-count condition for every starter.

Figure 2A adds a different view: the intermediate-glucose curves generally acidify earlier, while the tiger nut curves converge near pH 4 at the last plotted time, around 24 hours.[1] Exact acidification rates are not reconstructed here from the image. Endpoint counts and the time needed to acidify answer different development questions.

For a lower-sugar product brief, the useful finding is that no-added-glucose tiger nut samples still had measurable, substantial counts in this experiment—not that a no-added-sugar commercial drink has already been validated. “No glucose added” also does not mean “sugar-free.” Actual residual sugars, desired acidity, culture performance and the safety of the complete process must be established together.

## A higher result in one antioxidant assay is not an overall improvement

The same paper helps resist another common shortcut. Its tiger nut ORAC results showed a transient maximum at 24 hours, whereas TEAC decreased after fermentation; total soluble phenolics were generally similar to or lower than the unfermented samples, with an early exception for VEGE061.[1] These directions are visible in the original Figure 3 as well as discussed in the text.

Those measurements should remain separate. ORAC and TEAC are laboratory assays, not interchangeable measures of finished-product health value. A development team should not prolong fermentation to maximise one analytical result if the product's aroma, acidity or other measured attributes deteriorate. Nor should a supplier's botanical antioxidant description be treated as evidence of a benefit from the fermented beverage.

## Aroma is a screening signal, not a consumer preference verdict

The sensory work involved 11 untrained assessors scoring aroma descriptors on a 0–3 intensity scale.[1] This was not a large consumer liking study. A strong fruity note, for example, does not by itself establish that more consumers prefer the drink.

There is also a reporting issue worth keeping visible. Figure 5 uses a 0–100 radial scale, while the methods specify 0–3. The retrieved text does not explain the conversion. The discussion describes “lactic/buttery” notes, but the plotted axes are acid, sour, sweet, fruity, rancid, floral and herbal.[1] We therefore do not copy numerical radar scores or present “buttery” as a directly scored attribute.

The authors also flag rancid notes in the VEGE061 tiger nut sample, although the plotted rancid traces are low and closely grouped.[1] That is a reason to keep off-aroma assessment in the development programme—not evidence of a measured oxidation mechanism or a decisive flavour defect in every drink made with this starter.

A sensible sample comparison would keep the botanical base, sugar addition and processing consistent while the starter changes. Only after identifying a promising culture–base combination should the team judge whether changing flour grade or extraction is worth the accompanying reformulation. These are proposed development choices, not trials performed for this article.

## High counts after fermentation did not mean high counts after digestion

The study selected VEGE061 beverages for an INFOGEST 2.0 simulated digestion experiment. Figure 6 and the results text report a marked decline in recoverable counts for tiger nut and carob; rice retained higher post-digestion counts.[1] This finding is important precisely because it limits an attractive product story.

The methods collected the centrifuged supernatant—the bioaccessible fraction—after digestion and counted viable organisms there. They also introduced digestion fluids during the procedure.[1] The reported CFU/mL comparison is consequently not a direct mass-balanced survival percentage for all organisms initially present, and it is not a human colonisation or health-outcome test. No survival percentage is calculated here.

The paper did not include a storage-stability assessment, which the authors identify as a limitation.[1] It cannot establish a commercial shelf life, end-of-life viable count or validated probiotic claim for a supplied ingredient. A product intended to contain live cultures and a product intended to be heat-treated after fermentation are different briefs and need different evidence.

## Turn the research into a useful purchasing decision

For a first development round, ask for a material that can be described without relying on the words “premium,” “extra fine” or “fermented.” A useful request would read:

> We are evaluating a tiger nut base for a fermented beverage. Please identify the botanical material, whole or defatted form, milling or extraction process, any carriers or added sugars, and the delivered heat treatment. For flour, provide the particle-size method and distribution; for a liquid base, provide solids and relevant composition on the supplied basis. We will assess the candidate with our chosen starter in the intended formulation. Please quote the defined material and flag any specification or treatment still under development.

The decisive comparison is not “which supplier has the highest botanical content?” It is which well-defined material gives a workable combination of flavour, acidification, physical quality and composition in the intended process. The published study makes tiger nut a credible direction to investigate, but its count table, assay differences and digestion losses argue against promising all desirable outcomes from one fermentation setting.[1]

For an enquiry, share the target market, intended drink format, live-culture or post-fermentation heat-treatment plan, sugar target and available processing conditions. Those details make a [beverage application discussion](/solutions/beverages) and a [material quotation](/request-quote) more useful. Particular grades, documentation, stock and application performance remain subject to project-specific confirmation.
