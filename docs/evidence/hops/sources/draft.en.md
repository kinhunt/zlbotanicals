# Choosing hops for alcohol-free beer: aroma is not a bitterness specification

Research draft — not publication-approved. Independent source and bilingual editorial review required.

A request for “hop extract for a nonalcoholic drink” leaves the main purchasing decision unanswered. Is the ingredient supposed to add hop aroma, adjust bitterness, or support a light-stable beer design? Hopsteiner’s current technical sheets describe different products for those jobs: a fractionated oil developed for alcohol-free beer, an isomerized extract for bitterness adjustment, and a reduced bitter-acid extract with conditional protection against lightstruck flavor.[5][7][6]

The useful next step is not to rank those products by extract strength. It is to decide which job the beverage actually needs. This discussion focuses on alcohol-free and alcohol-reduced **beer**, where the retrieved supplier documentation and experiment apply. A clear hop-flavored sparkling water is a separate application; neither a beer specification nor a beer experiment establishes its finished-product performance.

## Start with the aroma fraction, not “more hops”

Hopsteiner describes Hop oil Type SELECTED FREE as conventionally extracted hop oil subsequently fractionated by distillation. The supplied product is diluted in propylene glycol, and the specification describes bittering substances as “not detectable.”[5] That is a materially different purchase from an extract intended to deliver iso-alpha acids. “Not detectable” is the supplier’s wording, not proof of absolute absence; the page does not give a detection limit for that entry.[5]

The same sheet characterizes the oil as containing mainly polar hop components and specifically positions it for alcohol-free and alcohol-reduced beers.[5] This makes composition and carrier identity more informative starting points than the generic name “hop extract.” A buyer seeking aroma without deliberately increasing bitterness has a reason to investigate that class of material, but no basis here to assume that any aromatic hop extract has the same composition.

The supplier recommends cold-side use, typically before filtration, and gives examples rather than one universal dose.[5] For a development brief, specify the actual beer base and addition point before discussing a replacement ratio. An oil delivered in propylene glycol should not be compared gram-for-gram with pellets, undiluted oil or an aqueous bitter-acid solution merely because all originate from hops.

## Low alcohol does not suppress every hop compound equally

A pilot-scale study by Cocuzza and colleagues provides a more useful picture than the blanket statement that hop aroma needs alcohol. The researchers started with a commercial, thermally dealcoholized wheat beer, adjusted ethanol content, and dry-hopped with Solero Type 90 pellets. They used 250 g/hl in 20-litre NC kegs, with semi-static contact for 14 days at 5°C.[3]

The dry-hopped groups contained 0.5%, 3.5%, 7.0% or 10.5% ABV, with three dry-hopped samples per group. Each alcohol level also had an unhopped control, and an original base beer was retained. Nagardo was added to all seventeen trial beers to limit microbial contamination.[3] These conditions matter: this was neither a zero-alcohol water experiment nor a commercial hop-extract comparison.

The aroma table shows very different patterns. Linalool changed relatively little across the dry-hopped groups, while beta-myrcene was much lower in the lowest-alcohol group. Isobutyl isobutyrate increased between the first two groups and then changed little. Geraniol did not rise monotonically: its highest-alcohol mean was lower than those of the two middle groups.[3]

These are the paper’s group-mean analytical values, not sensory scores.[3] The table on printed page 4 has two editorial defects: it is labeled “Table 3” while the prose calls it Table 4, and its heading ends at “μG/”. Surrounding aroma prose uses µg/l, providing context for the likely intended concentration unit; the incomplete heading is retained in the evidence archive rather than silently corrected.[3]

The authors suggest a sampling error as a possible explanation for the lower geraniol result at the highest alcohol content. That suggestion does not authorize replacing the measured value. They also attribute unusually high myrcene levels to a closed system and finished beer with few yeast cells, unlike processes that remove yeast or cold trub.[3] Do not turn these results into a universal recovery multiplier or a prediction of aroma intensity.

What the experiment supports is a narrower selection principle: alcohol dependence differs among hop constituents. It helps explain why a composition-led aroma choice deserves investigation. It does **not** independently validate SELECTED FREE, which was not the material tested.

## Bitterness adjustment is a different ingredient job

The supplier describes Isomerized Hop Extract 30% as an aqueous solution of potassium salts of iso-alpha acids for post-fermentation bitterness adjustment.[7] That is not the same functional brief as choosing a fractionated aroma oil.

Its application instructions also reveal why “water-soluble” can be an inadequate purchasing description. The sheet warns against prediluting the full-strength concentrate with beer because the lower pH causes precipitation; separately, it instructs addition into the beer stream with vigorous mixing.[7] Those statements are not contradictory: preparation of a concentrated premix and controlled dosing into a flowing bulk liquid are different operations. Do not reinterpret the warning as a proposed final-beverage pH target.

For a nonalcoholic project, establish whether bitterness is missing or already sufficient before adding an ingredient whose purpose is to increase it. Any dosage and sensory target still need assessment in the intended base; the retrieved documents do not supply a universal bitterness target for alcohol-free drinks.

## Light-stable bittering does not mean every flavor is stable

Tetra Iso-Extract is described as an aqueous solution of potassium salts of tetrahydro iso-alpha acids. The supplier expressly makes lightstruck-flavor protection conditional on the complete absence of alpha acids and conventional iso-alpha acids, and calls for avoiding contamination from equipment and yeast previously exposed to those materials.[6]

That is a whole-process qualification, not an additive that automatically makes any existing recipe light-stable. Nor is it a claim that every aroma constituent, color, or microbiological attribute will remain unchanged.

The aroma-oil sheet separately states that its aroma components remain stable during beer aging, but gives no finished-beer aging duration, light exposure or packaging comparison on the retrieved page.[5] Keep that supplier assertion distinct from a verified shelf-life study. It cannot settle whether a particular clear-bottle alcohol-free product retains its intended aroma.

## A more useful purchasing conversation

For an aroma-led alcohol-free beer, begin by asking which aroma fraction and carrier are supplied, what the lot-specific composition shows, and where the product is intended to enter the process. If bitterness also needs adjustment, treat that as a separate material decision. If lightstruck flavor is a concern, examine the full bittering system and process carryover rather than only the final addition.

The next development comparison should answer the unresolved product question, not reproduce the published pellet experiment by habit. Compare shortlisted aroma materials in the actual beer base under the intended process, while keeping bitterness adjustment explicit. Evaluate aroma character separately from bitterness and inspect the finished packaged product over the intended storage conditions. These are proposed development choices, not validated operating instructions.

The opportunity is therefore specific: **selecting an aroma-led hop material for low-alcohol beer without confusing aroma delivery, bitter-acid dosing and conditional light stability.** There is enough evidence for a bounded technical discussion, but not for a supplier ranking, a hop-water formula, a shelf-life promise or any health claim.
