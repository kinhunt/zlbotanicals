# Choosing pomegranate juice for beverages: fruit character, astringency and polyphenols can pull apart

Start a pomegranate beverage brief by deciding what to preserve: fresh-fruit aroma, a deep red appearance, or a pronounced drying finish. These are not interchangeable targets. A study of eight commercial pure pomegranate juices found different aroma profiles in from-concentrate and not-from-concentrate products, despite similar overall smell intensity; a separate tannase study reported rising acceptability alongside falling characteristic-flavour scores.[1][2]

A useful selection strategy is to choose the flavour direction first, then decide which juice and, if necessary, which astringency-reduction treatment fits it. A polyphenol specification describes an ingredient. It does not make that decision for you.

## First establish which pomegranate material is being compared

The 2021 commercial study examined eight 100% pomegranate juices: four from concentrate and four not from concentrate (NFC). It did not compare four undiluted industrial concentrates with four single-strength juices; it also recorded differences in fruit origin, cultivation, concentration and stabilization technology.[1] Its per-litre measurements refer to the purchased juices, not to specifications for concentrated ingredients.

NFC is not a fixed colour or sensory specification either. For example, a Sunmet NFC pomegranate juice technical sheet reviewed on January 7, 2025 specifies at least 14.5°Brix, acidity of 0.60–1.4% w/w as citric acid, bright dark-red colour, good clarity, and an acidic tang with sweetness.[5] Its turbidity limit is **below 100 NTU at 16°Brix**—the measurement condition belongs with the limit.[5] These are that supplier's documented requirements, not a universal NFC standard or measured results for a particular shipment.

For a clear pomegranate drink, select juice samples whose colour, clarity and flavour match the brief rather than relying on the NFC label alone. If the formula will also contain a pomegranate extract, evaluate the juice and extract separately. A single polyphenol percentage should not stand in for two different ingredient decisions. The studies below concern juice; they do not establish an equivalent beverage dose for peel extract.

## From-concentrate versus NFC can be a difference in character, not simply strength

Ten trained assessors evaluated the eight commercial juices using descriptive sensory analysis. They rated appearance, smell, mouthfeel and taste on 9-cm unstructured line scales; this was not a consumer preference ranking.[1]

In this sample set, NFC juices were more associated with pomegranate-fruit, fresh-fruit and beetroot notes. From-concentrate juices were more associated with dried-fruit, candy, caramel, honey and cooked notes, while overall smell intensity was similar between the groups.[1] Calling one group “more aromatic” misses the point: both can have a noticeable aroma, but communicate a different kind of pomegranate.

That observation suggests more useful sampling routes than declaring one format universally better:

- **Fresh-fruit-led refreshing drinks:** make recognizable pomegranate-fruit character the first selection criterion, then compare suitable juices in the actual formulation.
- **Cooked-fruit-led, darker-profile drinks:** consider dried-fruit, honey or caramel character part of the intended profile when appropriate, rather than automatically treating it as a defect.
- **Polyphenol-fortified drinks:** establish an acceptable juice base before evaluating the additional extract. Changing both the juice source and fortification material at once makes sensory differences harder to interpret.

These are proposed development routes, not three beverage formulas validated by the paper. The commercial products differed in origin, processing and packaging; their differences cannot all be assigned to concentration alone.[1]

## More total phenols does not necessarily mean more red pigment

In the commercial study, ICPJ7, a from-concentrate juice, had the highest total phenols at 3748.8 ± 19.8 mg GAE/L, but the sum of anthocyanins in Table 4 was only 0.8 ± 0.0 mg/L. NFC sample INCPJ8 contained 2614.0 ± 16.5 mg GAE/L total phenols and 280.6 ± 0.1 mg/L summed anthocyanins.[1] Total phenols were expressed as gallic acid equivalents, whereas anthocyanins were quantified using the paper's cyanidin-3-O-glucoside standard; these are not interchangeable quantities that can be subtracted from one another.[1]

The comparison shows why a total-phenol ranking cannot serve as a red-colour ranking. It does not measure the pigment loss caused by a particular process. The reported uncertainties come from two determinations, not variation across multiple production batches.[1]

NFC itself was no guarantee of pigment retention. Anthocyanins were not detected in INCPJ2, an HPP-treated NFC juice packaged in PET. The authors proposed residual enzyme activity and oxygen permeation as possible explanations, but this was not a controlled experiment holding juice, packaging and storage conditions constant.[1] Define the required finished-product colour directly rather than expecting “NFC” or “high polyphenols” to guarantee it.

## Reducing astringency can also change the pomegranate character

A 2025 tannase study illustrates a useful trade-off. The researchers used a single 25-kg batch of pomegranates and prepared juice while retaining what the paper calls “30% of the aril membrane and peel by weight.”[2] The denominator of that 30% is not further specified: it should not be rewritten as “the finished juice contained 30% peel,” nor assumed to describe an ordinary aril-only juice.[2]

Ten food-science faculty members and students evaluated four sensory attributes, each with a maximum of 10 points. In Table 4, extending treatment from zero to 90 minutes raised the taste score from 5.40 ± 0.3 to 8.53 ± 0.2 and acceptability from 4.48 ± 0.2 to 7.92 ± 0.3. However, characteristic-flavour scores fell from 7.79 ± 0.4 to 6.80 ± 0.4, and colour scores fell from 7.24 ± 0.6 to 7.02 ± 0.3.[2]

The ± values above are reproduced as published; the paper does not define them as standard deviations or standard errors for Table 4.[2]

The table therefore describes **improved acceptance with some loss of characteristic flavour**, not improvement in every attribute. The narrative says flavour and acceptability both improved, contradicting the table. The directions quoted here follow the actual table and should not be generalized to every pomegranate juice.[2]

The study also did not score astringency intensity as a separate sensory attribute: its “taste” row mixes descriptions such as “distinct bitterness” and “mild astringency.”[2] Bitterness and astringency deserve separate attention. The commercial-juice study's sensory lexicon defines bitterness as a caffeine- or quinine-like taste and astringency as a dry, puckering mouthfeel associated with tea leaves.[6]

If fresh pomegranate character is the aim, a deastringency trial should therefore consider what is being lost as well as whether acceptance rises. The tannase paper also has inconsistencies in concentration units and enzyme-inactivation conditions, so it is not an adequate basis for prescribing a production enzyme dose or time–temperature schedule.[2]

## Turn the purchasing question into a flavour brief

A practical next step is to compare candidate juices at the same target drinking strength, retaining a record of each sample's original acidity. A separate formulation round can then establish whether adjusting sweetness and acidity resolves the problem. If the first problem is the wrong aroma character, change the juice candidate before attempting to deastringe it.

A more useful purchasing brief might read:

> We are selecting juice for a fresh-fruit-led pomegranate beverage. The target is recognizable pomegranate-fruit aroma rather than a predominantly caramel or cooked profile. Please provide NFC and reconstituted from-concentrate samples separately, stating reconstitution strength, the basis used to express acidity, juice origin and treatments already applied. If a pomegranate extract is also offered, list its plant part, composition specification and proposed evaluation approach separately from the juice.

For a cooked-fruit-led product, substitute the desired dried-fruit, honey or caramel direction. The objective is not to identify an abstract “best pomegranate ingredient.” It is to choose the right flavour direction, then make explicit trade-offs among colour, drying mouthfeel and polyphenol specifications.
