# Pumpkin seed protein powder: what cold-pressed, finely milled and easy to mix actually tell you

A pumpkin protein powder can be sold as a daily protein serving, a simple kitchen ingredient or a smooth addition to coffee and oatmeal. Those are different reasons to buy, even when the product names look almost identical. Our three Amazon detail-page examples illustrate all three approaches: Sprout Living emphasizes cold pressing and protein per serving; Anthony’s emphasizes uncomplicated kitchen use; Micro Ingredients connects fine milling with everyday versatility.[1][2][3]

For a product developer, the useful question is not which phrase sounds most advanced. It is which promise the finished product needs to deliver. A powder that works in a blender smoothie has not thereby demonstrated rapid dispersion in a shaker or stability in a bottled drink. Pressing, milling and protein isolation describe different operations, while a mixing instruction describes use rather than a measured material property.[4][8]

## Three listings, three ways of making the same category useful

The original Amazon.com search returned eight rows. Two were different sizes of Sprout Living pumpkin protein, so eight rows should not be read as eight independent brands. Three distinct-brand detail pages were then examined; this is a small positioning sample, not a sales ranking.[11]

| Detail-page example | What the captured listing emphasizes | What a developer can learn from it |
|---|---|---|
| [Sprout Living, B01N7CLYX7](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N7CLYX7) | Cold-pressed pumpkin seeds; 20 grams of protein per serving; unflavored powder | Processing provenance and a recognizable protein-serving proposition lead the offer.[1] |
| [Anthony’s, B0CWVTCT7G](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CWVTCT7G) | No added sweeteners or fillers; smoothies, baked goods, water or milk | The powder is positioned as an ingredient the buyer can fit into an existing recipe or routine.[2] |
| [Micro Ingredients, B0FWJGBPWM](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FWJGBPWM) | Single-ingredient and raw wording; finely milled powder; 18 grams of protein per serving; smoothies, baking, oatmeal and coffee | Texture language and a wider set of everyday uses carry the proposition.[3] |

The distinction matters commercially. Sprout Living gives the buyer a protein-serving anchor. Anthony’s lets the buyer supply the flavor and choose the carrier. Micro Ingredients makes the powder sound usable beyond a conventional shake. These are interpretations of the captured copy, not evidence that the three products come from different industrial process routes.[1][2][3]

Nor do the examples support an 18-versus-20-gram nutrition ranking. Serving masses were not established in this text-only sample. Anthony’s captured record lists 30 servings but does not establish a protein amount per serving in its bullets; dividing net package weight by a serving count would not repair that gap. Sprout Living also has a structured “Protein Source: Blend” field beside pumpkin-focused title and bullet wording. That is a record-level inconsistency to resolve, not a basis for declaring either a blend or verified single-ingredient purity.[1][2][3]

For a new product, the opportunity suggested by these examples is to make one use occasion convincing rather than attach every possible use to the same powder. A kitchen ingredient can invite blending and baking without promising instant dissolution. A shake mix needs an explicit preparation method. A coffee addition creates a different sensory expectation again. The sample identifies those possible briefs; it does not tell us which would sell best.

## Pressing removes oil; milling makes powder; isolation separates protein

A supplier’s process description helps explain why “pumpkin protein” need not mean a purified protein isolate. Austrade describes its unroasted concentrate as mechanically partially defatted pumpkin seed material followed by pulverizing. Its roasted grade adds light salting and roasting before mechanical partial defatting, then further processing and milling.[8][9]

These descriptions distinguish three jobs:

- **Pressing or mechanical partial defatting** changes the seed material by removing part of its oil.
- **Milling or pulverizing** turns the remaining material into smaller particles. It does not by itself establish how much protein will dissolve.
- **Protein extraction and isolation** can involve dissolving protein under selected conditions, separating solids, precipitating the protein and drying the recovered fraction. The laboratory route discussed below includes those steps after the press cake has already been milled and further de-oiled.[4][8][9]

The point is not that every supplier must follow that laboratory route. It is that a retail claim about cold pressing cannot be substituted for evidence about protein isolation, and “finely milled” is not a particle-size distribution or a solubility result.

### A real supplier comparison: roasted and unroasted do not share an identical specification

Austrade’s public specifications make the distinction concrete.[8][9]

| Supplier-declared field | Organic Unroasted Pumpkin Protein Concentrate 60 | Organic Roasted Pumpkin Protein Concentrate 60 |
|---|---|---|
| Named seed material | Shine skin pumpkin, *Cucurbita moschata* | Austrian Styrian pumpkin, *Cucurbita pepo* var. *styriaca* |
| Protein | ≥60% | ≥57% |
| Moisture | ≤10% | ≤8% |
| Fat | ≤10% | ≤17% |
| Described sensory direction | Mild flavor | Distinctive roasted, nutty flavor; olive-green to brown powder |

These are published limits and supplier descriptions, not analyzed batches. The page specifications do not identify a dry-basis convention or protein assay method. In particular, the “60” in the roasted product name must not overwrite its ≥57% protein specification. The different species, processing descriptions and limits also mean this is not a controlled experiment proving what roasting alone changes.[8][9]

For recipe development, the useful difference is the intended sensory role. The roasted grade offers an explicit flavor direction for savory concepts; the unroasted grade offers a milder starting point. Neither page supplies comparative beverage dispersion measurements. Their fat limits also do not prove that any particular roasted batch contains more fat than any particular unroasted batch.[8][9]

An older Cambridge Commodities document demonstrates why the whole specification needs reading. Its February 19, 2020 sheet names “Organic Pumpkin Seed Protein 60%,” but the visible protein specification cell reads ≥80%. A separate nutrition page prints “65” in the protein row under “Amount per 100g,” without a unit attached to that entry. Its flowchart shows machine sieving before expression, followed by pumpkin seed cake, milling, packaging, storage and shipping. The process chart is useful evidence of a pressing-and-milling description; the unreconciled protein figures are not a reliable current purchasing specification.[6]

## Fine particles and soluble protein are different measurements

In a primary study of pumpkin seed press cake, researchers started with an already milled 300–600 µm fraction, further de-oiled it with hexane, then extracted protein under alkaline conditions and recovered it by precipitation and freeze-drying. That starting sieve fraction is specific to the experiment—not the mesh specification of any retail powder here.[4]

Original Table 1 reports 60.24 ± 0.05 g protein per 100 g untreated press cake and 68.68 ± 0.13 after further de-oiling; fat changes from 13.38 ± 0.12 to 0.77 ± 0.14 g/100 g. These are means ± half deviation range from duplicate measurements, not commercial grade definitions. The comparison illustrates enrichment by removing another component, before the separate protein-isolation stage.[4]

The researchers also compared alkaline extraction alone with ultrasound applied before, during or after extraction. Their solubility test used isolated protein at 5 mg/mL, two hours of agitation at room temperature, and centrifugation before measuring protein in the supernatant by Kjeldahl. It was not an instant-drink test.[4]

| Treatment | Protein solubility at pH 5 | At pH 7 | At pH 9 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Alkaline extraction control | 2.42 ± 0.06 a | 15.17 ± 0.20 a | 37.82 ± 1.07 a |
| Ultrasound during alkaline extraction, UAE | 6.59 ± 0.99 d | 18.80 ± 0.52 b | 46.87 ± 0.17 c |
| Ultrasound after alkaline extraction, AE+US | 4.90 ± 0.05 c | 23.07 ± 0.06 c | 46.93 ± 0.61 c |

Selected original Table 3 cells; values are percentages, means ± standard deviations, n=4 as reported. Different letters within the same column indicate a significant difference at p<0.05. Isolates were pooled from two runs on each extraction day before functionality analysis; n=4 should not be presented as four independently manufactured commercial batches.[4]

There is no single winning solubility treatment across these columns: UAE is higher than AE+US at pH 5, AE+US is higher at pH 7, and the two share the same significance group at pH 9. That is more informative than a blanket claim that treating protein after extraction always works best.[4]

The particle results also resist a simple “smaller is better” summary. Original Table 2 gives AE+US the smallest d90, 179.93 µm, but its d10 is 8.05 µm, higher than the control’s 4.82 µm. The measurements describe volume-based particle diameters in dispersions after alkaline extraction, measured at pH 7 after one day at 6°C. They do not characterize retail dry-powder mesh or prove that all parts of the distribution became smaller.[4]

A second primary study separates protein solubility from a water solubility index. Within its alkali-extracted samples, original Table 4 gives protein solubility of 10.32 ± 0.05% for untreated material, 9.75 ± 0.02% after conventional preheating and 15.99 ± 0.04% after microwave preheating (means ± SE, n=3). Its general prose suggests preheating improves solubility, but those cells show that conventional preheating did not improve this particular alkali comparison. The water-solubility-index preparation, meanwhile, used a 1:4 sample-to-water ratio and a day of shaking. Neither test establishes consumer mixing speed.[5]

Milling can still matter upstream. In a separate cell-culture-media study, researchers replaced a heat-generating coffee-grinder preparation with more thorough grinding cooled by liquid nitrogen before protein isolation. They reported higher protein recovery and a changed protein profile. Because grinding intensity and cooling changed together, the experiment cannot isolate a particle-size-only effect; it also cannot validate the mouthfeel or food suitability of a commercial drink powder.[10]

## Turn the retail promise into a specific development trial

The evidence suggests three different trial briefs, rather than one universal “mixability” score. These are proposed development approaches, not tested recipes:

**For a blender smoothie ingredient**, keep the smoothie base and blending procedure constant while comparing candidate powders. Evaluate flavor, perceived particles and thickening alongside protein contribution. This tests the kitchen-use promise closest to Anthony’s and Micro Ingredients’ positioning, without treating a smoothie as water.[2][3]

**For a shaker drink**, record wetting, visible lumps and sediment separately. Use a defined serving mass, liquid volume, temperature and shaking procedure. A powder can lose visible lumps yet still settle; the analytical soluble-protein fraction answers a different question from those observations. The laboratory methods above explain why a bare “solubility” number is inadequate without its conditions.[4][5]

**For a roasted savory or baked concept**, treat flavor as part of the ingredient’s role, not just a defect to mask. Start with the supplier’s actual roasted specification and judge the finished food for color, flavor and texture. Austrade’s description supports this as a plausible development direction, not as an already validated formula or a fixed substitution ratio.[9]

The strongest pumpkin-protein proposition is therefore a matched combination of material description, use occasion and preparation experience. The original listings show how brands frame that combination. Supplier documents and primary experiments show where the wording stops short of technical proof. Keeping those evidence types separate makes the commercial story more useful—not less.

## Sample and evidence scope

The marketplace sample is the original September 28, 2026 Amazon.com search of eight returned records and three distinct-brand details. Duplicate bullets were deduplicated for analysis only; original wrappers are retained. Packaging images and exact-ASIN labels have not been inspected for this candidate. Supplier limits remain supplier assertions; no purchase, batch COA, independent certification audit or finished-product trial was performed. Scientific results refer to the specified experimental materials, not the three retail products. Prices, review counts, market share and nutrition rankings are deliberately outside this analysis.
