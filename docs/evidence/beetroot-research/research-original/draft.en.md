# Why does beet-red plant-based meat stay red after cooking? Where sugar beet pectin and laccase fit

Making a plant-based patty look red before cooking is not the same task as making it turn an appropriate brown afterward. In a study by Sakai and colleagues, control patties containing beet red and methylcellulose remained visibly red after grilling; formulations combining laccase with sugar beet pectin showed a pronounced red-to-brown change.[30]

If a patty looks convincing raw but stays red when cooked, a more heat-resistant red ingredient is not necessarily the answer. The useful question is whether the formulation needs to preserve its color or change it during cooking. Two routes deserve separate consideration: adjusting the underlying shade and creating a reaction within the food matrix.

## Adjusting the shade is not the same as creating a color transition

A 2023 study by Lee and colleagues combined beet powder with caramel color, optimizing six responses together: L*, a*, and b* before and after cooking. L* describes lightness, while a* and b* are the red–green and yellow–blue axes.[23][30]

There is an important qualification. Despite “meat analogs” in the title, this experiment did not use a soy- or pea-based patty. The researchers repeatedly washed beef to remove myoglobin, then added the colors to that model.[23] It can help explain the interaction between two coloring ingredients. It does not establish a working inclusion rate for a plant-protein formulation.

The combination with the highest overall desirability contained 1.32% beet powder and 1.08% caramel color, with a desirability score of 0.65. Another combination contained 1.30% and 1.51%, respectively, with a score of 0.60.[23] Desirability is a multi-objective optimization score, not “65% similarity to beef.” The percentages are the study’s formulation levels, not pigment purity.

Nor did the study achieve an exact match in both states. For the first combination, Table 3 reports a* falling from 20.84 to 11.04 on cooking, compared with 21.76 to 6.69 for beef. Its b* rose from 19.39 to 25.18, whereas the beef value fell from 18.33 to 14.62.[23] Redness moved in the same direction; yellowness did not. The discussion favors the third combination when considering cooked appearance and lightness, while the conclusion recommends the first combination on overall desirability.[23] Those recommendations use different priorities.

The practical lesson is to avoid choosing a formula on raw redness alone. Decide whether caramel color fits the product brief, then consider raw appearance, cooked appearance, and the cut surface together. That is a suggested development approach—not a plant-based dosage recommendation derived from the beef model.

## Sugar beet pectin has a role beyond thickening

Sakai and colleagues’ 2022 work used a genuine plant-based system: pea-based textured vegetable protein, pea protein isolate, olive oil, and beet red. The named sugar beet pectin was GENU pectin type BETA BI-J; the laccase was Amano Enzyme’s LC-Y120.[30] These are the paper’s material identities, not evidence that any pectin or laccase can be substituted at the same level, or that a particular product is currently available.

Adding laccase to patties containing beet red and methylcellulose did not produce the pronounced browning seen when laccase and sugar beet pectin were used together.[30] The a* values in Table 1 make that distinction clear:

|Study formulation|a* before grilling|a* after grilling|
|---|---:|---:|
|Beet red + methylcellulose, without laccase|48.5 ± 0.9|47.9 ± 1.0|
|Beet red + methylcellulose + laccase|48.4 ± 0.6|47.0 ± 2.8|
|Beet red + sugar beet pectin + laccase|47.9 ± 2.4|9.3 ± 1.1|
|Beet red + methylcellulose + sugar beet pectin + laccase|48.3 ± 3.2|9.4 ± 1.6|

Values are transcribed from the original Table 1. The a* coordinate is dimensionless; the paper’s ± notation is retained without treating it as a guarantee of manufacturing-batch variability.[30] In this formulation, adding the enzyme and adding the enzyme together with sugar beet pectin were plainly different treatments.

The authors propose that ferulate-related structures in sugar beet pectin participate in laccase-mediated oxidation, helping the pigment reaction and connecting it with protein crosslinking.[30] This is a proposed reaction explanation, not a fully established industrial mechanism. Ordinary citrus or apple pectin should therefore not be presented as a validated substitute on this evidence.

Both laccase papers came from researchers affiliated with Amano Enzyme. They provide original experimental evidence, not independent replication by an outside team.[28][30]

Color was not the only property affected. The research also examined changes in hardness, chewiness, and cooking loss.[30] A promising color result still needs to meet the product’s texture brief; an instrumental texture result is not evidence of consumer preference.

## The useful reaction window is during heating

A 2024 follow-up examined reaction products in patties containing soy-based textured vegetable protein, pea protein isolate, sugar beet pectin, and beet red.[28] That is a different base from the 2022 pea-based formulation, rather than a repeat of an identical recipe.

In the follow-up, patties were cooked in an oven at 150 °C for 15 minutes. Their internal temperature reached about 85 °C at 10 minutes, when extracted residual laccase activity was no longer detectable.[28] Oven temperature, internal product temperature, and enzyme activity are distinct quantities. Figure 1 describes a reaction window under the study’s conditions, not a universal instruction to heat every product for 10 minutes.

The researchers extracted pigments from the patties and combined chromatographic retention-time comparisons with standards and solution reaction experiments. They proposed a pathway in which betanidin is cleaved to betalamic acid and cyclodopa, followed by formation of unknown brown polymers.[28] “Unknown” matters: the final brown material was not fully structurally identified and was not confirmed to be melanin.[28]

The authors also state that losses of brown compounds during molecular-weight cut-off treatment could not be quantified.[28] The chromatograms therefore do not establish an exact whole-system conversion yield. They should not be used to imply blanket food-use authorization for the resulting process, either.

## Choosing a useful next formulation trial

The following are development suggestions, not trials conducted for this article.

**If the main problem is a patty that is too pale or pink, while its cooking transition is already acceptable, start with shade adjustment.** The beet-powder/caramel study illustrates joint optimization of several color targets, but its myoglobin-removed beef matrix remains a major limitation.[23] Use the actual plant protein, oil phase, and cooking process rather than copying its optimum percentages.

**If the main problem is a red cooked interior, consider beet red, sugar beet pectin, and laccase as a research candidate—not a ready-to-use formula.**[30][28] Keep the existing formula as a control and distinguish pectin-only, enzyme-only, and combined treatments. Judge the outside and cut surface separately, so surface browning does not conceal an unchanged interior.

**If texture must stay within a narrow range, screen color and structure together.** The laccase–sugar beet pectin route concerns more than removing redness; the original work also discusses crosslinking, hardness, and water-holding-related behavior.[30] Define the acceptable texture range before deciding whether the color improvement is useful.

Keep the material identities and dose bases intact. Beet powder, a commercial beet-red preparation, and purified pigment are not interchangeable descriptions. In particular, supplementary Table S1 of the 2022 study reports laccase as 50 U/g TVP—not 50 U/g finished patty.[31] This article does not convert that research dose into a production recommendation. The specific enzyme preparation, target-market conditions of use, labeling, and product safety process still require separate confirmation.

The most useful outcome is not a universal percentage. It is a sharper formulation question: **does the product need a better background shade, or a controlled color transition during cooking?** Keeping those tasks separate prevents ingredient heat stability, finished appearance, and cooking safety from becoming the same decision. A brown appearance should not replace the product’s own time–temperature safety validation.
