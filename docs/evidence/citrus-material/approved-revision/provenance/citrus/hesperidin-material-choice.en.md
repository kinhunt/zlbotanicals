# Hesperidin or glucosyl hesperidin? Choose the material before comparing the assay

A buyer looking for a citrus flavonoid can encounter ordinary hesperidin, an enzymatically modified glucosyl hesperidin powder, and a protein-based hesperidin delivery system. These are not three interchangeable purity grades. One changes the molecule; another changes the surrounding formulation.[1][2] The useful purchasing question is therefore not simply “Which has the highest percentage?” It is “Which material fits the product, the declared composition and the intended market?”

For a water-based product where dissolved material matters, food-grade glucosyl hesperidin deserves a separate evaluation rather than being treated as a more expensive version of ordinary hesperidin. For a product whose brief specifically requires unmodified hesperidin, changing to the glucosylated material changes that brief. Neither route removes the need to qualify the actual finished formulation.

## Three names, three different starting points

**Hesperidin** is a flavanone glycoside: hesperetin attached to the disaccharide rutinose. **Hesperetin** is the aglycone, without that sugar portion. **Monoglucosyl hesperidin** retains the hesperidin structure and has an additional glucose unit. In a primary synthesis study, researchers first generated several glucosylated forms with CGTase and then used glucoamylase to obtain the mono-glucoside.[1] Buying hesperetin is not the same as buying a water-soluble hesperidin grade.

The distinction is commercially visible. NAGASE's CITRAPEAK product page identifies the material as glucosyl hesperidin and specifies monoglucosyl hesperidin at 75.0–85.0% on a dry basis, alongside total hesperidin of not less than 70.0% on a dry basis.[3] Those are two analytical descriptions of the same product, not percentages to add together. EFSA's compositional table explicitly describes its “total hesperidin” measurement as a calculation expressed as hesperidin.[6]

The EU novel-food specification is also instructive: it includes 75.0–85.0% monoglucosyl hesperidin and 10–20% hesperidin, both on a dry basis.[7] Unreacted hesperidin within that range is part of the defined material, not automatically evidence of a defective batch. These are specifications for the identified regulatory material, not specifications for every powder sold under a similar name—and not ZL specifications.

For quote comparison, keep the analytes separate. Ask which result represents monoglucosyl hesperidin, which represents unreacted hesperidin, and which is an equivalent-based total. Request the method, reference standard and dry-basis versus as-supplied basis. Comparing “80% glucosyl hesperidin” with “95% hesperidin” without those definitions is not a meaningful purity ranking.

## Better aqueous performance does not make every experiment a beverage test

There is a sound material rationale for evaluating glucosyl hesperidin. The 2022 primary study found a much more water-favouring octanol–water partition behaviour for the mono-glucoside than for hesperidin or hesperetin.[1] But a partition coefficient is not a saturation solubility in grams per litre. The same paper's antioxidant and cell experiments are not evidence that a finished beverage produces a health benefit.

EFSA's 2024 opinion reports applicant measurements of the specified novel food's water solubility at three temperatures, as well as aqueous stability tests across several pH conditions.[6] This is useful characterization, but it is a public assessment of submitted data—not an independently repeated head-to-head trial of all commercial grades. The underlying proprietary stability reports were not available for direct review here.

One result is especially relevant to a formulation decision. In tests of 1% w/v aqueous solutions over up to four weeks, EFSA reports MGH and hesperidin as stable under the tested conditions except after four weeks at 55°C and pH 7, when recovery was 88%.[6] This supports considering the material for aqueous development; it does not establish universal heat resistance. It also measures chemical recovery, not every aspect of appearance, flavour, microbiology or package compatibility.

The official cosmetic brochure makes a striking solubility comparison, but the displayed table does not state a test temperature or a complete method.[4] It should not become a numerical performance chart for a food-grade ingredient. For sample selection, the directly identified food grade and its technical dossier are the better starting point.

## A protein complex is a different development route

A 2023 primary paper improved hesperidin's measured aqueous performance by co-precipitating polyphenols with sodium caseinate, soy protein isolate or whey protein isolate, with trehalose used in relevant preparations. The process involved alkaline dissolution, acidification and freeze-drying; it was not simply stirring hesperidin into a protein drink.[2]

Its solubility assay used pH 7 phosphate buffer, 24 hours of stirring, centrifugation, filtration and subsequent HPLC quantification. Whey protein gave the highest reported hesperidin solubility among the tested protein co-precipitates.[2] That establishes a research route worth investigating where a protein-containing material fits the product. It does not establish instant dissolution in an acidic clear drink, a commercially available grade, or equivalence to glucosyl hesperidin.

The practical choice is whether the brief allows molecular modification, a protein-containing delivery system, or neither. Start there rather than asking a supplier to make all three options match a single “water-soluble hesperidin” line item.

## Food and cosmetic grades must stay separate

NAGASE explicitly distinguishes cosmetic-grade Alpha Glucosyl Hesperidin for topical application from food-grade CITRAPEAK for oral intake.[5] The cosmetic brochure lists the INCI name Glucosyl Hesperidin and is directed to cosmetic-industry customers.[4] A matching ingredient name does not, by itself, justify substituting that grade into a food formula.

For a beverage, request the food-grade specification and the documentation applicable to the destination market. For a toner or serum, request the cosmetic-grade dossier and assess the actual formula. Cosmetic suggested-use levels are not beverage doses, and topical study findings do not establish oral benefits. This article does not claim that ZL stocks, distributes or manufactures either named branded material.

## The EU authorisation changes the sourcing decision

An EFSA safety opinion and a market authorisation are different documents. The Commission authorised glucosyl hesperidin through Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/167.[7] Its annex names **soft drinks marketed in relation to physical exercise** and **energy drinks**, each with a maximum level of 525 mg/L. It separately covers food supplements, excluding infants and young children, at 115 mg/day for children between 3 and 10 years and 200 mg/day for the general population older than 10, with specified labelling requirements.[7] These are legal limits for the listed uses, not recommended formulation targets or effective doses.

That is narrower than the manufacturer's general applications list, which includes many beverage and confectionery formats.[3] A technical application list is not permission to use an ingredient in every listed food category in every country.

There is also a sourcing restriction. Article 2 reserves placing this novel food on the EU market to Nagase Viita for five years from 20 February 2025, unless a subsequent applicant obtains the described authorisation without relying on protected data or with Nagase Viita's agreement. The corrigendum explicitly adds 20 February 2030 as the data-protection end date.[7][8] The retrieved Union-list consolidation dated 16 March 2026 retains these conditions.[9] Matching a chemical assay alone therefore does not establish an eligible EU supply route. Ask how the proposed supply is covered; do not assume a generic substitute qualifies.

The legal specification deserves its own check. The 2025 authorisation lists lead and arsenic each at no more than 0.1 mg/kg, whereas the earlier EFSA opinion's specification table lists 2 mg/kg for lead and 1.5 mg/kg for arsenic.[6][7] Do not copy the assessment's table into an EU purchasing specification as if it were the final legal entry. Check the complete applicable entry and subsequent changes before launch; the March 2026 consolidation is a dated reference, not a guarantee that no later amendment exists.

## A practical shortlist, not a universal winner

**If the brief requires unmodified hesperidin:** keep that identity fixed and compare qualified hesperidin materials against the intended dosage form. Do not silently exchange molecular identity for a more attractive solubility claim.

**If the brief needs an aqueous food ingredient:** shortlist a documented food-grade glucosyl hesperidin, subject to destination-market and supply-route eligibility. Then compare the actual sample in the finished matrix. The manufacturer identifies both aqueous applications and the grade's composition; that is a reason to evaluate it, not a finished-product guarantee.[3]

**If the brief deliberately includes a protein delivery system:** the co-precipitation study can inform development, but obtain evidence for an actual producible material before treating the research preparation as a sourcing option.[2]

A useful enquiry states the intended country, product category, desired chemical identity, target finished-product concentration and process conditions. Ask for the corresponding grade and analytical definitions before negotiating a kilogram price. The commercial objective is a material that can be sourced, used and described correctly—not the highest assay number on an incomparable quotation.
