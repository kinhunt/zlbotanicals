# Buying monk fruit: how to read °Brix, mogroside V percentages and 1:1 sugar-replacement claims

A monk fruit quotation may describe a liquid concentrate, an enriched powder or a finished sweetener blend. Their headline numbers refer to different properties. FruitSource® lists **65±5 Brix** and **3.30–3.70% mogroside V** on the same concentrate specification.[4] Monk Fruit Corp names its MFC-E50 powder **50% Mogroside V Extract**.[2] Lakanto markets its Classic erythritol–monk fruit blend as a **cup-for-cup sugar replacement**.[8] These are a refractometer reading, a compound-specific specification and a consumer measurement instruction, respectively—not three points on a purity scale.

Reading the denominator changes the purchasing comparison. A concentrate brings its own colour and storage requirements; an extract percentage identifies only part of the composition; a cup-for-cup blend includes ingredients beyond monk fruit. The following examples show how those distinctions appear in actual documents.

## Four commercial documents and what their numbers describe

The comparison concerns published supplier and brand information, not laboratory testing or a representative market sample. FruitSource's specification was approved on 20 September 2021 and is used here as a historical document, rather than a current release specification.[4]

| Documented material | Published specification or declaration | Meaning of the headline number |
|---|---|---|
| FruitSource® Monk Fruit Concentrate, AG6522 | Amber liquid; “Total Solids”: 65±5 Brix by refractometer; “Total Mogroside V”: 3.30–3.70% by HPLC. Ingredient: Luo Han Guo concentrate.[4] | The Brix reading and V assay are separate fields. Neither is a complete sugar profile. |
| Monk Fruit Corp MFC-E50 | Powder grade named 50% Mogroside V Extract; the page lists V grades from 25% to 70%.[2] | 50% refers specifically to V, not the complete composition or the powder's sweetness in a finished drink. |
| Matakana Monk Fruit Powder | 90% monk fruit extract, with “standardised to a minimum of 7% mogrosides” in parentheses; 10% maltodextrin.[5] | 90% is an ingredient proportion. The 7% declaration refers to mogrosides, without identifying V separately. |
| Lakanto Classic Monkfruit Sweetener with Erythritol | Erythritol and monk fruit extract; marketed to measure cup for cup like sugar.[8] | 1:1 is a volume-based replacement instruction for the finished blend. The page does not disclose the extract percentage. |

## Concentrate: read Brix and the V assay separately

FruitSource's sheet assigns different methods to its two specifications: a refractometer for the field labelled “Total Solids” and HPLC for mogroside V.[4] A buyer therefore needs both fields. Treating 65 Brix as 65% V would substitute one measurement for another; treating it as a measured sugar composition would go beyond the data supplied.

The same sheet permits light to dark amber liquid and specifies refrigerated storage at 0–5°C, in a properly sealed container away from direct sunlight, with a stated shelf life of 12 months.[4] Those details affect a beverage project as directly as the assay. For a water-clear drink, colour should be evaluated at the intended dilution. For a fruit-coloured drink, the permitted amber appearance may be acceptable. The refrigerated specification also belongs in the handling-cost comparison, using current instructions for the exact product.

The sheet does not identify the V percentage as dry-basis or as-supplied.[4] That distinction must be resolved before converting purchased concentrate mass into grams of V. Keep any such calculation separate from the amount needed to sweeten the beverage: equal V mass is not evidence of equal perceived sweetness.

## Powder: distinguish extract proportion from compound content

Matakana's product page calls the material monk fruit juice powder and declares “Monk fruit extract 90% (standardised to a minimum of 7% mogrosides), maltodextrin 10%.”[5] Two levels of composition appear in that sentence: the ingredients used in the powder and the mogroside standardisation associated with the extract.

The parentheses attach the standardisation to the extract in the ingredient declaration. They do not provide a V-specific assay or a complete analytical reporting basis. Multiplying 90% by 7% would therefore not establish the finished powder's V content. A V-based purchasing comparison needs a V-specific specification for the supplied powder, with its reporting basis stated.

MFC-E50 uses a different kind of number: its name identifies 50% mogroside V.[2] It should be compared with other V specifications on a matched analytical basis, rather than ranked against Matakana's 90% ingredient proportion. A carrier declaration and a compound assay answer different questions; a declared carrier is not itself evidence of adulteration.

Selective purification also differs from simply drying a juice. In a 2011 laboratory study, dried fruit powder was extracted twice with boiling water. The subsequent HZ 806 resin separation used a deionized-water wash followed by 40% aqueous ethanol to elute the target compound.[6] This illustrates separate extraction and purification operations, not the manufacturing route or residual-solvent result of any named commercial product. The [monk fruit ingredient guide](/plant-extracts/ingredients/monk-fruit) covers processing and material forms in more detail.

## Finished blend: read 1:1 as a measurement instruction

Lakanto Classic lists erythritol and monk fruit extract and describes its use as cup-for-cup replacement of sugar.[8] Here, the unit matters: one cup of the blend replaces one cup of sugar under the brand's instructions. This does not disclose how much extract is in the blend or establish a gram-for-gram replacement for concentrated monk fruit powder.

That makes it a different purchasing item from an enriched extract. A developer seeking a concentrated sweetening component still has to design the rest of the formula; someone seeking a ready-to-measure sugar replacement is evaluating the whole blend. Matakana's declared 10% maltodextrin and Lakanto's erythritol–extract composition belong in those respective material descriptions, rather than being collapsed into one generic “filler” category.[5][8]

Taste descriptions require a separate reading. Monk Fruit Corp describes its concentrate as having a clean sweet taste without bitterness; FruitSource's sheet says only “Sweet.”[1][4] These supplier descriptions do not constitute a controlled comparison. They identify claims to evaluate in the intended food, alongside sweetness intensity, background flavour and lingering taste.

## One extract did not have one fixed sweetness multiplier

A 2018 study tested 16 sweetener systems with 40 healthy adults. Its monk fruit sample was an **extract containing 50.6% mogroside V**, not isolated V. Solutions were prepared with filtered water, and participants rated sweetness on a general labelled magnitude scale (gLMS), holding a 15 mL sample for five seconds and rating it before expectorating.[7]

The paper's model-derived equal-sweetness concentrations changed with the sucrose reference. Concentrations below are expressed as grams of the respective sweetener per 100 mL of solution (% w/v); the monk fruit column refers to the extract, not to V alone.[7]

| Sucrose reference, % w/v | Model-derived monk fruit extract concentration, % w/v | Reported sweetness potency relative to sucrose |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.0191 | 262 |
| 10 | 0.0694 | 144 |
| 15 | 0.141 | 106 |

*Source: Wee et al. (2018), Table 4; one 50.6%-V extract tested in water. These are model-derived comparisons, not recommended beverage doses.[7]*

Sweetness potency is the sucrose concentration divided by the extract concentration at matched sweetness. The three ratios are approximately 262, 144 and 106: the reported multiplier falls as the reference sweetness rises.[7] A single “times sweeter” figure therefore leaves out a condition essential to interpreting this extract's performance.

The study measured sweetness intensity, not consumer liking, aroma or aftertaste. Its authors also distinguish results in water from performance in foods and beverages.[7] For purchasing, this supports comparing samples at matched perceived sweetness in the actual base, rather than using the table to calculate a commercial dose or to convert between concentrate and powder.

## Compare cost after establishing the required sample dose

A useful sample brief identifies the beverage or food base, the material form under consideration, the current composition and V reporting basis, and the colour and storage requirements. Keep the comparison tied to an exact product and specification version. For spoonable replacements, include whether the formula calls for volume-based measurement of a finished blend.

Once trials establish an acceptable dose, ingredient cost per batch is the required mass of the supplied ingredient multiplied by its price per unit mass. Handling and storage costs can then be added on the same batch basis. Comparing equal V mass can help examine composition, but it cannot replace that sensory and application step.

The four documents provide a practical sorting rule: compare Brix with Brix, V assays on a matched basis, ingredient proportions with ingredient proportions, and finished blends as complete systems.
