# Buying monk fruit: why 65 °Brix, 50% mogroside V and a 1:1 sugar replacement are different specifications

A liquid concentrate, a high-mogroside powder and a spoonable sugar replacement can all arrive under the name “monk fruit.” Their headline numbers answer different questions. One describes a refractometer reading, another the concentration of a named compound, and another how the brand intends consumers to measure a finished blend.

This distinction is visible in actual commercial documents. A FruitSource® concentrate specification lists **65±5 Brix** alongside **3.30–3.70% mogroside V**. Monk Fruit Corp lists an **MFC-E50, 50% Mogroside V Extract**. Lakanto describes its Classic erythritol–monk fruit product as a **cup-for-cup sugar replacement**.[4][2][8] Those figures are not competing measures of “purity.” Before comparing quotations, identify which kind of material each supplier has priced.

## Four commercial examples, four different things to buy

These are observations of supplier or brand documents, not tested samples, a representative market survey or an endorsement. The FruitSource document is dated 20 September 2021; it is a useful published specification example, not confirmation of today's release specification or stock.[4]

| Documented material | What the source actually states | What the buyer should not infer |
|---|---|---|
| FruitSource® Monk Fruit Concentrate, item AG6522 | Light to dark amber liquid; “Total Solids,” measured by refractometer, 65±5 Brix; “Total Mogroside V,” measured by HPLC, 3.30–3.70%; ingredient list: Luo Han Guo concentrate.[4] | 65% mogroside V, 65% measured sugars, or colourless performance in a clear drink. |
| Monk Fruit Corp MFC-E50 | A powder extract grade identified as 50% mogroside V; the same page lists grades from 25% to 70% V.[2] | 100% isolated mogroside V, a full composition declaration, or a guaranteed sweetness multiplier for a finished drink. |
| Matakana Monk Fruit Powder | Ingredients: 90% monk fruit extract, described as standardised to a minimum of 7% mogrosides, plus 10% maltodextrin. The description also calls it monk fruit juice powder.[5] | 90% mogroside V, 7% mogroside V, or a carrier-free powder. |
| Lakanto Classic Monkfruit Sweetener with Erythritol | Two listed ingredients: erythritol and monk fruit extract; marketed to measure cup for cup like sugar.[8] | A cup of pure extract, a disclosed extract percentage, or identical behaviour to sucrose in every recipe. |

The useful comparison is not “which brand has the biggest number?” It is **which specification describes the ingredient function you need**.

## Read the concentrate on two axes, not one

The FruitSource document is unusually helpful because it puts a solids-related reading and a compound assay on the same page, with different analytical methods.[4] Treat them as separate acceptance fields. A refractometer result cannot stand in for the HPLC result, and neither field supplies a complete sugar profile.

For a beverage developer, the amber appearance is also a real selection issue: the published specification explicitly permits light to dark amber liquid.[4] If the brief is a water-clear drink, ask for an agreed colour test at the intended dilution rather than assuming the word “juice” means visually neutral. If the brief welcomes a fruit-coloured or botanical appearance, the same characteristic may be acceptable. That is an application decision, not a quality ranking.

Handling belongs in the comparison as well. This particular document specifies refrigerated storage at 0–5°C in a properly sealed container away from direct sunlight, with a stated shelf life of 12 months.[4] Do not transfer those conditions to every monk fruit concentrate—or assume a dry extract and this concentrate can share the same storage plan. Obtain the current instructions for the exact product, including what changes after opening.

The published concentrate sheet does not explicitly identify the percentage assay as dry-basis or as-supplied. Before converting a quoted drum mass into grams of V, resolve that basis in writing. A missing basis is a question to close, not permission to choose the interpretation that makes the offer look better.

## Purification and drying solve different problems

A high-V powder is not adequately described as “juice with the water removed.” In an original separation study, dried fruit powder was first extracted twice with boiling water; the subsequent HZ 806 resin process used a deionized-water wash followed by 40% aqueous ethanol to elute the target compound.[6] Water extraction and selective separation were distinct operations in that experiment.

This paper supplies a concrete reason to ask about the production route. It does **not** establish how any of the commercial products above were manufactured, and it did not validate their flavour. Likewise, seeing an ethanol elution step in a paper does not tell you the residual solvent in a delivered ingredient. Ask for the supplier's actual route and relevant specification rather than importing a laboratory protocol into the purchase order.

There is a second distinction inside the word “pure.” A product can be an extract without an added carrier while still containing several native compounds. Conversely, a product with a high percentage of “monk fruit extract” can have a much lower percentage of any particular mogroside. On Monk Fruit Corp's page, “50%” refers specifically to V; on Matakana's page, “90%” refers to the extract ingredient fraction.[2][5] Those denominators must stay visible.

## A carrier is not the same as a bulk sweetener

Matakana's declared maltodextrin and Lakanto's declared erythritol illustrate two different composition questions, not two interchangeable versions of “filler.” Matakana lists 10% maltodextrin in a powder whose other ingredient is monk fruit extract; Lakanto sells an erythritol–extract blend intended for cup-for-cup measurement.[5][8]

For procurement, separate three lines:

- **Extract identity:** which mogrosides are measured, by which method, and on what reporting basis?
- **Added ingredients:** what is present besides the extract, and in what proportion?
- **Intended function:** is the purchased material a concentrated sweetening component, a handling premix, or a finished bulk sugar-replacement system?

The Matakana wording is especially instructive. “Minimum 7% mogrosides” does not say “7% mogroside V.” Nor does the page settle every analytical-basis question needed to calculate the final powder's V content.[5] Do not quietly multiply 90% by 7% and report the result as a verified V assay. Ask whether the standardisation belongs to the extract input or the finished powder and request a V-specific result if the project requires one.

A declared carrier is not evidence of adulteration. The procurement failure would be approving one composition and receiving another, or comparing two products as though their declared compositions were the same.

## “Flavour” needs a separate description

A material's taste, a named flavour ingredient and a sweetener blend are not synonyms. The commercial sources above establish different forms and compositions; they do not provide a controlled sensory ranking of all four materials. Monk Fruit Corp describes its concentrate as having a clean sweet taste without bitterness, while the FruitSource specification gives the much narrower sensory description “Sweet.”[1][4] These are supplier descriptions, not proof that either product will be neutral in your tea, dairy base or acid drink.

Ask what “flavour” means in the quotation. Is the supplier describing the extract's own sensory character, offering a separate flavour preparation, or proposing a blend containing flavouring? Request the full ingredient declaration before treating these as equivalent offers. The documents reviewed here do not establish a general composition for products sold as “monk fruit flavour.”

Keep sweetness intensity, botanical character and unwanted finish as separate acceptance questions. A single V number cannot answer all three; the purchase decision should not depend on translating “clean taste” into an unmeasured promise.

## Why one sweetness multiplier cannot set the purchase quantity

A 2018 original study offers a more useful check than a universal “times sweeter” claim. It tested 16 sweetener systems with 40 adults, using filtered-water solutions and sweetness ratings on a general labelled magnitude scale. The monk fruit ingredient was **an extract containing 50.6% mogroside V**, not isolated pure V. Participants held a 15 mL sample for five seconds and rated sweetness before expectorating.[7]

The paper's model-derived equal-sweetness concentrations changed with the sucrose target:[7]

| Sucrose reference, % w/v | Monk fruit extract concentration, % w/v | Reported sweetness potency relative to sucrose |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 0.0191 | 262 |
| 10 | 0.0694 | 144 |
| 15 | 0.141 | 106 |

The numbers concern that extract and that water-test design. They are **not commercial beverage dosing recommendations**, a concentrate-to-powder conversion, or a test of liking, aroma or aftertaste. Their purchasing value is narrower and stronger: the same extract did not have one fixed relative sweetness across the target range.[7]

For a quotation comparison, therefore, distinguish **equal marker mass** from **equal perceived sweetness**. The first is a composition calculation; the second needs product-specific sensory evidence. Neither can be replaced by comparing two supplier headline multipliers obtained under unspecified conditions.

## Turn the distinction into a better sample request

Instead of asking for “your best monk fruit,” state the decision:

> We are comparing a liquid concentrate and an enriched powder for a specified beverage base. Please identify the exact product, current specification version, full ingredient composition, mogroside V result and reporting basis, other available mogroside and sugar data, colour specification and storage conditions. We will establish the required sweetness in our own base before comparing ingredient cost per batch.

For a dry-mix or spoonable replacement, add the intended measurement basis and whether a premixed bulk ingredient is wanted. For a flavour-sensitive product, define which background note or finish is unacceptable instead of simply requesting “higher purity.”

Only after an acceptable sample dose is established should a buyer multiply the **mass of the supplied ingredient actually needed** by its quotation and account for the project's handling requirements. No market price or universal saving follows from the public documents above.

The practical result is a shorter, more meaningful shortlist: compare concentrates against the concentrate brief, enriched extracts against the high-intensity sweetening brief, and declared blends against the finished-system brief. The name “monk fruit” starts the discussion; the material identity closes it.
