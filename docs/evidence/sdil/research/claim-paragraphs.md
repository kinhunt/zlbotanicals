# Fact-bearing paragraph index / 事实引用段索引

Every cited paragraph below is copied in full from the draft. Source IDs retain the inherited ledger mapping. Editorial suggestions and hypothetical examples are not product evidence.

## en.md

### Paragraph 3 | sources 1,4,7

A packaged milk tea is not exempt from the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy (SDIL) simply because it contains milk. Under current HMRC guidance, the milk-based exemption requires at least 75ml of qualifying milk per 100ml of prepared drink; plant-based milk substitutes have a separate set of conditions.[4] The government plans to remove the exemptions for milk-based and milk-substitute drinks with added sugar and lower the entry threshold from 5g to 4.5g per 100ml for chargeable events from 1 January 2028.[1][7]

### Paragraph 4 | sources 2,8

Those are different rule sets. HMRC’s July 2026 documents still describe draft legislation and forthcoming final guidance, rather than an expansion already in force.[2][8] For a tea brand, importer or formulation team, the useful starting point is therefore the finished product’s composition and supply format—not a presumed tax saving from changing a sweetener.

### Paragraph 7 | sources 1,2,4,6,7

| Question | Current rules checked in September 2026 | Published plan for chargeable events from 1 January 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| What is the lower sugar threshold? | At least 5g and less than 8g total sugars per 100ml for the lower band; at least 8g for the higher band, for drinks otherwise within scope.[4] | Lower threshold of 4.5g; the higher threshold remains 8g. Eligible lactose would be disregarded when assessing milk-based drinks.[2][7] |
| Are milk-based drinks exempt? | The exemption requires at least 75ml qualifying milk per 100ml prepared drink.[4] | The milk-based exemption would be removed for drinks with added sugar; a lactose allowance would replace it in assessing sugar thresholds.[1][2] |

| What about café drinks? | Simply dispensing into a cup, glass or takeaway cup does not make the operator a packager. Separate rules cover liable machine flavour concentrates.[4] | The policy says open-cup beverages remain unaffected; this does not erase existing concentrate rules.[1][4] |
| Which rates should a team use? | From 1 April 2026, £2.08 per 10 litres in the lower band and £2.78 per 10 litres in the higher band.[4][6] | Do not treat the 2026 rates as fixed 2028 rates: HMRC says band rates are announced at budgets and take effect the following April.[2] |

### Paragraph 8 | sources 1,2,5,7

The important timing distinction is between an announcement, draft legislation and an operative rule. The November 2025 response moved the proposed implementation date from 1 April 2027 to 1 January 2028.[5] The July 2026 tax information note says legislation will be introduced in Finance Bill 2026–27, while the detailed policy paper anticipates enactment in Finance Act 2027.[1][2] The draft’s commencement clauses refer to **chargeable events**, not simply the date a recipe is approved or a drink is manufactured.[7]

### Paragraph 10 | sources 4

HMRC’s general test covers added sugar or qualifying sugar-containing ingredients, at least 5g sugar per 100ml in the ready-to-drink or diluted product, the specified drink/preparation formats, packaging and an alcohol content of 1.2% ABV or less; applicable exemptions must also be considered.[4] The sugar threshold is not a standalone rule that makes every sweet beverage taxable.

### Paragraph 11 | sources 4

For the milk exemption, HMRC includes animal milk, reconstituted or recombined milk, fermented milk, buttermilk and specified whey forms; cream is not included in its definition of milk.[4] A formulation record should consequently distinguish actual qualifying milk from cream, creamer and the overall quantity of dairy-derived ingredients. “Contains dairy” is not a substitute for that record.

### Paragraph 12 | sources 4

A packaged tea below the 75ml-per-100ml milk condition may already need assessment under the current general rules.[4] Conversely, a drink meeting that milk condition should not have the proposed 2028 treatment applied early. These are product-scope observations; the business’s registration position and any other applicable reliefs need their own review.

### Paragraph 14 | sources 4

The current milk-substitute conditions include at least 120mg calcium per 100ml, a plant origin, all or most of the same uses as milk, a similar consistency and no carbonation.[4] An oat ingredient appearing in the recipe does not establish that the finished tea drink meets all those conditions. Nor does a fruit or botanical drink become a milk substitute just because it is plant-based.

### Paragraph 17 | sources 2,5

The published policy would assess relevant milk-based drinks using total sugars after deducting allowable lactose; it includes lactose from liquid milk and milk products such as milk powder.[2] The 2025 response expressly says that the allowance would apply irrespective of the drink’s percentage milk content.[5] The present 75% milk exemption should therefore not be carried into a future worksheet as a minimum condition for lactose allowance.

### Paragraph 18 | sources 2,7

Not all dairy-associated sugars would qualify. The policy excludes separately added lactose or galactose and hydrolysed lactose; the draft also addresses lactose converted, or to be converted, by added lactase.[2][7] A lactose-free milk description is consequently not proof that all sugars originating in that milk would be disregarded.

### Paragraph 19 | sources 2,7

**Whey requires a reserved decision.** The policy paper says lactose in whey powder is excluded, but draft regulation 4C qualifies its whey exclusions by whether the constituents are recombined or the whey is reconstituted in the drink.[2][7] These passages are not sufficiently aligned to turn into a universal rule for whey-containing milk tea. This article does not determine allowance eligibility for a specific reconstituted-whey formulation; that point needs final guidance or clarification from HMRC or a qualified UK tax adviser.

### Paragraph 20 | sources 2

HMRC’s proposed evidence framework includes recipe quantities, supplier specifications for typical lactose content, manufacturing information, final nutrition declarations and available analyses with their methodology and interpretation.[2] The paper also states that laboratory analysis cannot currently identify a product’s free-sugars content directly.[2] A total-sugars value on a label can support the file, but cannot on its own establish which lactose qualifies for the allowance.

### Paragraph 22 | sources 2

For plant-based milk substitutes, the published policy would leave a drink outside scope where sugars come only from one core plant ingredient, such as oats or soya.[2] Where two or more plant-based ingredients contribute sugars, liability would instead be assessed against total sugar content; adding rice syrup to rice milk is an explicit example, even though both ingredients come from rice.[2]

### Paragraph 23 | sources 2

That does not make every multi-ingredient plant drink taxable. The same policy allows a single-core-ingredient milk substitute to be mixed with fruit or vegetable juice while remaining outside scope, whereas sugars from a milk substitute added to plain animal milk are treated as added sugars, with the lactose allowance then relevant to the assessment.[2] Read these exceptions alongside the general statement, rather than counting plant names on the ingredient list.

### Paragraph 25 | sources 2,4,7

|---|---|
| Packaged milk tea meeting the current qualifying-milk condition | Check the present exemption separately from the proposed removal and lactose allowance.[4][2] |
| Packaged milk tea below that condition | Review current scope now; do not assume the first possible exposure is in 2028.[4] |
| Single-core-ingredient oat milk substitute, with no other sugar contribution | Check the proposed single-ingredient exclusion and the finished product’s classification.[2][7] |

### Paragraph 27 | sources 4

HMRC currently lists drinks sold as powder among the exclusions, while liable packaged flavour concentrates used in dispensing machines have been covered since 1 April 2023.[4] A dry tea ingredient, a bottled finished tea and a machine flavour concentrate should therefore not share one assumed classification. The concentrate rule is defined by its use in a dispensing machine and the resulting drink, not simply by the word “concentrate” on a specification.[4]

### Paragraph 28 | sources 7

The 2028 draft also contains a specific drinking-yoghurt exclusion: at least 90ml fermented milk per 100ml prepared drink, with no other liquids added for the purpose of making it more drinkable.[7] Adding yoghurt to a tea drink is not enough to establish that exclusion. Products close to this boundary need the actual formulation and process reviewed against the final text.

### Paragraph 30 | sources 2

The immediate deliverable is a traceable record of the product as supplied and consumed. HMRC’s proposed lactose evidence categories provide a useful foundation, but the worksheet below is an editorial template, not an official form.[2]

### Paragraph 33 | sources 4

A sweetener candidate can be recorded as a formulation option without making it the policy’s presumed answer. HMRC’s current guidance does not classify stevia as sugar for SDIL purposes, but this is not a finished-formula approval, a statement of permitted use or evidence of sales growth.[4] Treat tax scope, ingredient-use compliance and formulation performance as separate decisions. This article makes no forecast for stevia demand.

### Paragraph 36 | sources 2,8

The official pages retrieved for this article still present the expansion as a draft-led process and say final guidance will follow.[2][8] This review has not established an enacted post-consultation expansion or resolved the whey wording; it is not an exhaustive parliamentary or legal-status opinion. Recheck the enacted provisions, commencement, final HMRC guidance and product-specific evidence before relying on a 2028 classification.

## zh.md

### Paragraph 3 | sources 1,4,7

预包装奶茶含有牛奶，并不意味着一定豁免英国软饮料行业税（Soft Drinks Industry Levy，SDIL）。按照HMRC现行指南，乳饮豁免要求每100ml调制后的饮品至少含75ml符合定义的乳；植物乳替代饮料则有另一组条件。[4] 英国政府计划取消含添加糖乳饮及乳替代饮料的相应豁免，并将起征糖含量门槛从每100ml的5g降至4.5g，拟适用于2028年1月1日起发生的应税事件。[1][7]

### Paragraph 4 | sources 2,8

现行规则和未来方案必须分开看。HMRC于2026年7月发布的材料仍属于草案及配套说明，并表示最终指引将另行公布，不能据此把扩围写成已经实施。[2][8] 对茶饮品牌、进口商和研发团队而言，首先需要弄清的是成品配方和供应形态，而不是先用更换甜味剂来推算税款节省。

### Paragraph 7 | sources 1,2,4,6,7

| 判断事项 | 2026年9月核对的现行规则 | 拟适用于2028年1月1日起应税事件的方案 |
|---|---|---|
| 糖含量门槛 | 对其他条件也符合的饮品，低档为每100ml总糖≥5g且<8g，高档为≥8g。[4] | 下限拟降至4.5g，高档门槛仍为8g；乳饮判断门槛时拟扣除符合资格的乳糖。[2][7] |
| 乳饮豁免 | 每100ml调制后的饮品至少含75ml符合定义的乳，才符合这一乳饮豁免条件。[4] | 拟取消含添加糖乳饮的豁免，改为在糖含量判断中适用乳糖扣除额。[1][2] |
| 植物基饮品豁免 | 符合乳替代饮料条件才可适用该项豁免，并非所有植物来源饮品均可适用。[4] | 含添加糖的乳替代饮料拟纳入；仅由单一核心植物原料释放糖的情形，公布方案仍保留范围外处理。[2] |
| 门店现配杯饮 | 仅向杯、玻璃杯或外带杯分装饮品，不使经营者成为包装商；机器用应税风味浓缩液另有规则。[4] | 政策称开放杯饮品不受此次变化影响，但这不取消既有浓缩液规则。[1][4] |
| 税率年份 | 2026年4月1日起，低档为每10升£2.08，高档为每10升£2.78。[4][6] | 不能将2026年税率直接当作2028年确定税率；HMRC说明各档税率在预算中公布，并于随后4月生效。[2] |

### Paragraph 8 | sources 1,2,5,7

政策宣布、草案发布和规则生效，是三个不同阶段。2025年11月的咨询答复已把原拟2027年4月1日的实施日期推迟至2028年1月1日。[5] 2026年7月的税务影响说明表示，相关立法将纳入Finance Bill 2026–27；详细政策文件则预期在Finance Act 2027中完成立法。[1][2] 草案的适用日期以**应税事件（chargeable event）**为准，不能简化为配方批准日或生产日。[7]

### Paragraph 10 | sources 4

HMRC的一般适用条件涉及生产时添加糖或符合定义的含糖原料、即饮或稀释后每100ml至少5g糖、规定的饮用或调制形式、包装，以及不超过1.2%的酒精体积分数；还需考虑适用豁免。[4] 因此，达到某个糖含量，并不单独决定所有甜饮都要纳税。

### Paragraph 11 | sources 4

在乳饮豁免中，HMRC将动物乳、复原或重组乳、发酵乳、酪乳及规定的乳清形态纳入乳的定义，但不包括奶油。[4] 配方记录应区分符合定义的乳、奶油、植脂末或奶精，以及乳来源原料的总添加量。“含乳制品”不能代替这一区分。

### Paragraph 12 | sources 4

低于每100ml含75ml乳条件的预包装茶饮，现在就可能需要按一般规则评估，不能以为所有奶茶都要等到2028年才与SDIL有关。[4] 反过来，符合现行乳饮条件的产品，也不应提前套用未来取消豁免的方案。这里讨论的是产品范围；企业是否需要注册以及是否有其他可用减免，仍须分别判断。

### Paragraph 14 | sources 4

现行乳替代饮料条件包括：每100ml至少含120mg钙、源自植物、具有乳的全部或大部分用途、稠度与乳相近，并且不含气。[4] 配方用了燕麦原料，并不能证明成品茶饮满足上述全部条件；果汁或植物提取物饮料，也不会仅因“植物基”这一名称就成为乳替代饮料。

### Paragraph 17 | sources 2,5

公布方案拟对相关乳饮采用“总糖扣除符合资格的乳糖”后判断门槛，液态乳及奶粉等乳制品中的乳糖可纳入考虑。[2] 2025年咨询答复还明确，乳糖扣除额不以饮品的乳含量百分比为前提。[5] 因此，现行75%乳饮豁免条件，不应被带入未来记录表，变成享有乳糖扣除的最低乳比例。

### Paragraph 18 | sources 2,7

但并非所有与乳原料有关的糖都可扣除。政策说明排除了单独作为原料添加的乳糖或半乳糖，以及已水解的乳糖；草案也涉及因添加乳糖酶而已经或将要转化的乳糖。[2][7] 原料名称标有“无乳糖”，不能证明其中所有源自乳的糖都可从门槛判断中扣除。

### Paragraph 19 | sources 2,7

**含乳清的配方需保留人工复核。** 政策说明笼统表示乳清粉中的乳糖不纳入扣除，但草案第4C条又对乳清成分是否在饮品中重组、乳清是否在饮品中复原作了限定。[2][7] 两处文字尚不足以支持一条适用于所有含乳清奶茶的结论。本文不判定具体复原乳清配方的扣除资格；这一点应等待最终指引，或向HMRC、具备英国税务资质的专业顾问核实。

### Paragraph 20 | sources 2

HMRC拟采用的证据框架包括配方用量、供应商出具的典型乳糖含量规格、制造过程、成品营养声明，以及已有检测结果和方法解释。[2] 文件同时指出，目前不能通过实验室分析直接识别产品的游离糖含量。[2] 营养表上的总糖数值有参考价值，却不能独自证明哪些乳糖符合扣除条件。

### Paragraph 22 | sources 2

对于植物乳替代饮料，公布政策拟将仅由单一核心植物原料——例如燕麦或大豆——贡献糖的饮品保留在征税范围外。[2] 如果两种或更多植物原料贡献糖，则需按总糖含量判断；米饮中额外加入米糖浆就是文件列举的例子，即使两种原料都源于稻米，糖浆仍被视为添加糖原料。[2]

### Paragraph 23 | sources 2

这并不意味着“多一种植物成分就一定纳税”。同一政策允许单一核心植物原料贡献糖的乳替代饮料与果汁或蔬菜汁混合后仍处于范围外；但若把植物乳替代饮料中的糖加入纯动物乳，这部分糖会被视为添加糖，再考虑乳糖扣除额。[2] 这些例外必须与一般表述一起阅读，不能只数配料表里出现了几种植物名称。

### Paragraph 27 | sources 4

HMRC现行指南将以粉末形式销售的饮品列为排除项，而用于饮料分配机的应税包装风味浓缩液，自2023年4月1日起已有相应规则。[4] 干态茶原料、瓶装茶饮和机器用风味浓缩液，不能共用一个未经核查的分类。浓缩液规则看其在机器中的使用方式及调制后的饮品，而不是规格书中是否出现“浓缩”二字。[4]

### Paragraph 28 | sources 7

2028年草案还列出特定的饮用酸奶排除条件：每100ml调制后的饮品至少含90ml发酵乳，且不得为了提高可饮用性而加入其他液体。[7] 奶茶里加了酸奶，不足以证明符合该条件。接近这条边界的产品，应以真实配方和加工过程对照最终文本。

### Paragraph 30 | sources 2

眼下最有用的准备，是让供应状态、饮用状态和配方组成可以追溯。HMRC拟提出的乳糖证据类别可作为整理依据；下方工作表是编辑制作的空白模板，不是官方申报表。[2]

### Paragraph 33 | sources 4

候选甜味原料可以进入研发记录，但不必成为政策的预设答案。HMRC现行指南不将甜菊列为SDIL定义中的糖，不过这不等于成品配方获得批准，也不证明具体使用合法或销量将增长。[4] 税务范围、原料使用合规和配方表现应分别判断；本文不预测甜菊需求。

### Paragraph 36 | sources 2,8

本次读取的官方页面仍把扩围放在草案推进阶段，并表示最终指引将后续公布。[2][8] 本次核验没有确认到咨询后的已成法扩围文本，也未解决乳清措辞差异，不构成穷尽议会记录的法律状态意见。依赖2028年分类作商业决定前，应重新核对已通过条文、生效安排、最终HMRC指引和具体产品证据。
