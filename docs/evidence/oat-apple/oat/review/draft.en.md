# Oat beta-glucan for drinks: do not buy molecular weight in isolation

A higher reported molecular weight is not enough to predict which oat beta-glucan ingredient will make a drink thicker. In one published comparison, the commercial oat material with the higher reported molecular weight had the lower initial assay viscosity. The products contained other oat components, and the measured viscosity changed after laboratory digestion. That is a useful warning for buyers: a molecular-weight number describes the polymer, not everything that happens when the supplied powder meets water. [Wolever et al., 2020, Table 1 and methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469033/)

For a drink project, the purchasing decision is therefore not simply “high or low molecular weight.” It is whether to introduce a bran-rich material, a more soluble oat concentrate, or a material whose beta-glucan has deliberately been shortened—and whether that choice still makes sense after the intended process. Ingredient identity, beta-glucan content and the behaviour of the prepared drink need separate answers.

## A historical assay comparison—not a current ingredient ranking

Wolever and colleagues studied two commercial sources, identified at the time as OatWell (OP1) and PromOat (OP2), alongside four experimentally modified materials. Each beta-glucan preload in the human study supplied 4 g of oat beta-glucan added to 200 mL water; the control was water alone. This required 13.7 g of OP1 or 11.90 g of OP2; equal beta-glucan amounts did not mean equal powder amounts or equal accompanying nutrients. [Full paper, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469033/)

| Historical study material | Reported beta-glucan molecular weight, kDa | Initial assay viscosity, cP | Post-digestion assay viscosity, cP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| OP1: OatWell | 1,980 ± 265 | 25 ± 1 | 112 ± 9 |
| OP2: PromOat | 841 ± 110 | 117 ± 7 | 143 ± 7 |

Values are the paper’s means ± standard deviations from quadruplicate measurements—not current supplier specifications. The molecular-weight method used chromatographic separation with calcofluor detection. The supplement reports initial viscosity after 5 minutes at 37°C and 160 rpm in a Rapid Visco Analyser, followed by 120 minutes under those conditions with the enzymes. These are assay conditions, not a beverage-processing recommendation. “Post-digestion” means an **in-vitro assay with amylase and protease**, not viscosity measured inside participants. [Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469033/); [supplementary methods](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/nutrients/nutrients-12-02275/article_deploy/nutrients-12-02275-s001.pdf)

There is an important reporting limitation. The supplement describes the viscosity slurry as 0.5 g of sample in 25 mL buffer and calls its solid-to-liquid ratio the same as the human-study preparation. That does not match the total ingredient masses in Table 1 if “sample” means the whole commercial powder. The publication does not resolve this wording. Use the values as the authors’ **reported assay comparison**, not as a reconstruction of the viscosity of the drinks participants swallowed. The table is a reason to request matched-method data, not a purchasing specification or a brand league table. [Supplementary methods](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/nutrients/nutrients-12-02275/article_deploy/nutrients-12-02275-s001.pdf)

The practical distinction survives that limitation: the paper does not support using molecular weight alone to rank different oat preparations by viscosity. Nor can a 2020 material identity establish the formulation or specification of every grade sold under the same name today.

## What an actual supplier offers is more specific than “oat extract”

Lantmännen’s current public PromOat documentation describes a soluble oat-bran fibre with very little insoluble fibre. Its product leaflet lists **Standard, Gluten-free, Instant and Gluten-free organic** variants. These are real commercial distinctions, but the leaflet does not provide a molecular-weight specification for each one. “Instant” should not be translated into “low molecular weight” without supporting documentation. [Manufacturer product page](https://www.lantmannenbiorefineries.com/food-ingredients/products/oat-beta-glucan/promoat/); [product leaflet](https://www.lantmannenbiorefineries.com/siteassets/5.-food-ingredients/ingredienser/havrefiber/3.-promoat/promoat_product-leaflet_241015.pdf)

The percentage wording also matters. The page says beta-glucan content is **“up to 34%”**, and **“up to 29%”** for the gluten-free version. The leaflet says the gluten-free product “contains 29%.” Neither document supplies a complete, batch-linked purchasing specification with analytical basis and tolerance. A buyer should not convert a portfolio maximum into a guaranteed minimum, or assume that every variant has the same concentration. [Product page](https://www.lantmannenbiorefineries.com/food-ingredients/products/oat-beta-glucan/promoat/); [leaflet, pages 1–2](https://www.lantmannenbiorefineries.com/siteassets/5.-food-ingredients/ingredienser/havrefiber/3.-promoat/promoat_product-leaflet_241015.pdf)

For beverages, the manufacturer emphasizes added viscosity, creaminess and smoothness, with specific wording about mildly acidic and refrigerated applications. Its broader product page and leaflet also call PromOat acid and heat stable. These are supplier descriptions, not a published time–temperature–pH envelope or proof of molecular-weight retention through your process. They justify asking for a suitable grade and application data; they do not establish that a thin, clear, ambient-stable acidic drink is an easy substitution. [Manufacturer applications](https://www.lantmannenbiorefineries.com/food-ingredients/products/oat-beta-glucan/promoat/applications/)

## A thicker oat base can release less beta-glucan

The distinction between polymer and ingredient is especially important when comparing a concentrate with an oat flour or bran-based base.

Grundy and colleagues examined oat flakes, flour milled from those flakes, and a beta-glucan-rich bran material called BG32. Their laboratory study compared untreated and hydrothermally processed materials, incubating them in buffer at 37°C and measuring beta-glucan release, molecular weight and the flow behaviour of recovered supernatants. It was not a finished-beverage shelf-life trial. [Grundy et al., 2017, full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388193/)

Cooking reduced the release of beta-glucan from the flour and flakes in that experiment, while increasing the viscosity of their recovered liquid fractions. The authors linked this apparently contradictory result to the starch-rich matrix and residual particles. Enzyme treatments helped separate the contributions: amylase had the greatest effect on the viscosity of the cooked flour and flake samples; lichenase, which depolymerizes beta-glucan, produced a major viscosity drop in BG32. [Results, sections 3.2–3.4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388193/)

The sourcing implication is not “avoid cooking.” It is that **a thick oat base is not an assay of dissolved, intact beta-glucan**. A flour-rich sample and a soluble concentrate can reach a similar apparent thickness for different reasons. If the project needs beta-glucan enrichment rather than simply more body, approving the thicker beaker can select the wrong criterion.

The same paper also reported late molecular-weight loss during prolonged incubation of some flour and flake samples and discussed residual beta-glucanase activity. The enriched BG32 material, processed to inactivate that enzyme, behaved differently. This does not predict losses during a particular factory hold; it shows why enzyme history belongs in the material discussion rather than being treated as irrelevant background. [Results, section 3.3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388193/)

## Enzyme-thinning is not one interchangeable operation

If an oat drink is too thick, “use enzymes” is an incomplete development instruction. Starch hydrolysis and beta-glucan depolymerization address different contributors to the system.

In the Wolever study, the lower-molecular-weight test materials were not simply the original bran with a new number on its specification. The preparation involved a beta-glucanase-containing enzyme, extrusion, incubation, hot-water extraction, centrifugation, recovery of the soluble fraction, freeze-drying and milling. Composition and material structure therefore also changed. Those samples cannot establish what an arbitrary commercial “low-MW oat beta-glucan” will do, or isolate chain shortening from every other processing effect. [Supplementary preparation method](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/nutrients/nutrients-12-02275/article_deploy/nutrients-12-02275-s001.pdf)

For development, distinguish two briefs. **Reducing starch-driven body while preserving the selected beta-glucan profile** calls for evidence about the enzyme preparation and the actual processed matrix. **Deliberately shortening beta-glucan to change flow** calls for a new material qualification, including the resulting molecular-weight profile and beta-glucan content. These are proposed decision paths, not validated recipes. Neither brief is satisfied by a viscosity result alone.

## Make the first ingredient trial answer a purchasing question

Rather than screen a long list of powders at the same powder addition, start with the material contrast that matters to the product.

**For an oat-forward, creamy drink:** compare the existing bran/flour route with a soluble-concentrate candidate at a matched, analytically supported beta-glucan input. Keep the original base as a reference. Differences in total solids, starch, protein and insoluble matter are part of the ingredient choice—not noise to hide. Then check whether the desired texture remains after your heating, cooling and holding sequence.

**For a drink that must remain lightly bodied:** set the acceptable drinking texture before asking for the highest molecular weight. A supplier’s thickening benefit may be your formulation constraint. Consider a lower-viscosity candidate only with clarity about whether the difference comes from concentration, hydration, accompanying solids or deliberate polymer degradation. The studies above explain why those routes cannot be treated as equivalent.

**For an existing enzymatically processed oat base:** qualify the beta-glucan ingredient in contact with the actual base and process, not only in water. Ask which enzymes are used, when they are inactivated and what evidence supports the resulting beta-glucan profile. Do not assume either preservation or degradation from the word “enzymatic.”

These comparisons are formulation recommendations derived from the evidence, not manufacturer performance promises. The approval package should connect an identifiable supplied grade to beta-glucan content and test basis, molecular-weight method where relevant, and viscosity measured at a stated concentration, temperature, hydration time and shear condition. Finished-drink composition and texture remain separate acceptance decisions.

## Keep a formulation result separate from a health claim

The 2020 study enrolled 16 healthy participants. Neither the predefined primary endpoint, glucose peak rise, nor the secondary endpoint, glucose incremental area under the curve over 0–120 minutes, differed significantly among treatments. Findings for early response and time to peak involved additional, post-hoc comparisons. The study was supported by DSM and VTT and included employees of those organizations among its authors. It cannot be used to promise a glucose benefit for a newly formulated beverage, or to set a universal molecular-weight efficacy threshold. [Full paper, results, limitations and disclosures](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469033/)

The commercially useful brief is narrower and more actionable: **which supplied oat ingredient delivers the intended beta-glucan amount and drinking texture after this process?** Bring the serving size, desired body, base composition, enzyme steps, thermal route and destination market into the sourcing discussion. Confirm the proposed grade and its documents before treating a literature material—or a headline percentage—as the ingredient you are buying.
