# Tamarind seed gum for food: choose the hydration route before comparing viscosity

Start a tamarind seed gum enquiry with the production process, not with a request for the “highest viscosity.” Socius lists GLYLOID®2A as hot-water soluble, GLYLOID®3S as cold-water soluble and Glyate® as a cold-water-soluble, low-viscosity grade.[2] Those distinctions make the first buying question practical: **which grade can develop the required texture within the available mixing, heating and holding sequence?**

## Seed gum is not tamarind fruit flavour

MP Gokyo describes tamarind seed gum as a material obtained from the seeds of *Tamarindus indica*, which are rich in the neutral polysaccharide xyloglucan. The same page separately describes culinary use of the fruit in pastes, sauces and beverages.[1] An enquiry for a sour tamarind flavour and an enquiry for a texture modifier should not use “tamarind extract” as their only material definition.

The manufacturer's page also presents GLYLOID® for food and beverages and TAMAVISCO® for personal care.[1] Request documentation for the exact proposed grade and destination market rather than treating the two ranges as interchangeable.

## Three published grades, three selection questions

Socius, a distributor, describes the following grades on its product page.[2] These are third-party product descriptions, not a comparison of measured performance.

| Published grade | Distributor's description | What to check before sampling |
|---|---|---|
| GLYLOID®2A | Hot-water soluble. “Heat to a minimum of 80°C for at least 15 minutes.”[2] | Can the process accommodate this preparation step? This is a hydration instruction, not a food-safety heat treatment. |
| GLYLOID®3S | Cold-water-soluble grade for applications without a heating process.[2] | Ask for preparation instructions, including mixing time and equipment, for the intended cold process. |
| Glyate® | Cold-water soluble; “Low-viscosity grade.”[2] | Establish whether the objective is thickening or another function. Cold solubility alone does not establish equivalent thickening. |

For a cold-blended sauce, first check whether the candidate can hydrate within the available mixing time. For a cooked fruit preparation, compare the stated heating requirement with the actual temperature and hold time. These descriptions help narrow a sample shortlist; they do not supply an equal-dose replacement ratio.

## Make viscosity figures comparable

A 2019 study extracted a polysaccharide from Yunnan tamarind seeds using acidic hot water and ethanol precipitation. Before rheological analysis, the researchers stirred the samples at 80°C for one hour and then rested them for 12 hours.[3] The material was a laboratory preparation, not an identified GLYLOID®2A, GLYLOID®3S or Glyate® sample; this preparation does not demonstrate cold-process performance.[3]

The study reported a Newtonian plateau at low shear rates and shear thinning at higher rates and concentrations. Its flow measurements used increasing and decreasing shear-rate sweeps between 1 and 1,000 s⁻¹ at 25°C.[3] MP Gokyo's general description, by contrast, includes “Stable viscosity with Newtonian fluid.”[1] The materials and test conditions have not been matched, so these descriptions do not establish a contradiction—or a common flow specification for all tamarind seed gums.

Before comparing viscosity quotations, ask for:

- **Concentration and basis:** mass per volume or mass per mass, and whether the concentration refers to the supplied powder or a specified component.
- **Preparation:** water or formula composition, mixing conditions, hydration temperature and time, and rest period.
- **Measurement:** temperature, instrument and geometry or spindle, and shear rate or rotational speed.

In this paper, concentrations expressed as **% w/v refer to the mass of the prepared TSP material per solution volume**, not a xyloglucan purity specification; 2% w/v means 2 g per 100 mL of solution.[3] Keep that basis when quoting the results rather than silently converting them to a weight percentage in a food recipe.

## Heat resilience is not constant viscosity while hot

For a 2% w/v TSP solution at a shear rate of 2 s⁻¹, the study reports apparent viscosity falling from 3.01 to 0.13 Pa·s as temperature rose from 5 to 85°C. The authors also report that the heating and cooling curves coincided well, which they interpreted as thermal resilience.[3]

These observations answer different questions. Recovery on cooling does not mean unchanged viscosity during hot processing. The experiment heated and cooled at 5°C per minute; it did not test an industrial hot-fill, retort or shelf-life programme.[3]

For sample evaluation, separate the texture needed during hot transfer or filling from the texture needed after cooling. A satisfactory cooled sample answers only the second question.

## Check acid, salt and sugar in the intended formula

The study tested 2% w/v TSP in buffers at pH 1, 4, 7, 10 and 13, measuring flow at 25°C. The authors described apparent viscosity as almost unchanged across pH 1–10 and significantly lower at pH 13.[3] Those were discrete test points, not measurements at every pH in the interval. The paper also notes an earlier study with a different response to decreasing pH.[3]

Salt and sucrose were examined separately, again at 2% w/v TSP and 25°C. The conditions included 0.5 M and 1.0 M NaCl, 1.0 M KCl and 1.0 M CaCl₂, and sucrose at 10%, 20% and 30% w/v. The authors report lower apparent viscosity with the salts, to an extent depending on ion type, and a slight increase with sucrose.[3] Here, M denotes mol/L; the sucrose percentages are mass per solution volume, not °Brix.

These results justify investigating acidic applications, but they do not establish a dosage or stability outcome for a complete sauce. The authors identify interactions with food components as further work.[3] Test the shortlisted grade with the intended acid, salt, sugar and other ingredients, including their actual order of addition.

## “Emulsification” needs more than a viscosity result

Socius's two-page bulletin says Glyloid “can help stabilize sauces and dressings without the need for ingredients such as PGA.” It provides no specified oil phase, homogenization procedure, droplet-size data or matched comparison formula for that claim.[6] Treat it as a supplier application proposal, not demonstrated replacement equivalence.

A project aimed at changing pour behaviour has a different acceptance criterion from one aimed at preventing oil separation. For the latter, request results from a suitably matched emulsion. The 2019 study evaluates solution rheology, not a controlled PGA-replacement trial.[3]

## What to include in a sample request

Send the finished-product type, target market and actual preparation sequence: cold blend or heated preparation, mixing equipment, available temperature and hold time, and when acid, salts and other ingredients enter. State the required outcome—pour behaviour, mouthfeel or emulsion stability—and how it will be assessed.

Request the exact grade, composition documentation, preparation instructions and viscosity method together. Compare samples in the intended process before negotiating substitution on price alone. Confirm market-specific food-use and labelling requirements for the proposed material separately from its functional suitability.
