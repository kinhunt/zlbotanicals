## Independent primary-source checks

### 1. Commercial identity and exact grade wording

Read original manufacturer HTML and distributor HTML, not only flattened extraction. Manufacturer identifies seed origin/xyloglucan and presents food/beverage GLYLOID® separately from personal-care TAMAVISCO®. Its exact general phrase is “Stable viscosity with Newtonian fluid.” Neither statement supplies an independently validated grade-level performance specification.[1]

Distributor table is unambiguous:[2]

- **GLYLOID®2A** — “Hot water soluble”; “Suitable for applications requiring heat processing. Heat to a minimum of 80°C for at least 15 minutes.”
- **GLYLOID®3S** — “Cold water soluble”; “Cold water-soluble grade for applications without a heating process.”
- **Glyate®** — “Cold water soluble”; “Low-viscosity grade.”

The correction does not silently turn Glyate® into GLYLOID® Glyate, 3S into a low-viscosity grade, or a solubility label into an emulsification guarantee. The 80°C/15-minute statement is distributor preparation guidance, not a kill step. No original grade TDS, batch COA or hands-on trial was available; none is implied.

### 2. Extraction versus preparation before testing

Original JATS §3.2: peeled seeds milled to 80 mesh; pretreatment with 80% ethanol at 25°C for six hours; citric-acid extraction at pH 3.5 and 80°C for 20 minutes; ethanol precipitation and vacuum drying. Original JATS §3.7 separately says all rheology samples were stirred at 80°C for one hour and rested for 12 hours.[3] These are different stages, not competing hydration instructions. Rest temperature is not specified; the article does not invent it or require all commercial grades to rest for 12 hours.

### 3. Concentration, shear and viscosity denominators

- §3.7 gives TSP concentrations 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0 and 10.0% **w/v**. This is the prepared extract material in solution, not assay purity or an industrial food-use dose.[3]
- Rheometer: Discovery HR-3, 40-mm flat plate, 1,000-µm gap; five-minute temperature equilibration before measurements. Flow sweeps 1→1,000→1 s⁻¹ at 25°C. Oscillation uses 0.1–100 rad/s at 2% strain—**not** the same quantity as steady-shear s⁻¹.[3]
- §2.3 and Figure 3 caption support the Newtonian plateau/shear-thinning account. No exact universal concentration or critical-shear threshold is asserted in the article.[3]
- Independently parsed both original JATS tables into cells and compared their structure with the archived table extraction. Table 1's η₀ is fitted zero-shear viscosity at the flow-test conditions, not the 5°C/2 s⁻¹ apparent viscosity. No table flattening, purity calculation or performance ranking enters the article.[3]
- §2.6 explicitly gives **2% w/v TSP, 2 s⁻¹, 3.01→0.13 Pa·s during 5→85°C**. §3.7 specifies 5°C/min heating and cooling, with separate fixed shear rates 2, 60 and 200 s⁻¹. The cited number pair belongs to 2 s⁻¹ only.[3]
- The source calls viscosity decline “gently” and broadly praises thermal stability, but those adjectives do not erase its numerical temperature dependence. The article uses the reported numbers and the authors' recovery interpretation, not a hot-viscosity or shelf-life guarantee.[3]

### 4. Acid, salt and sugar

Methods give 2% w/v TSP for pH 1/4/7/10/13 and for salts/sucrose. Figures 5 and 7 captions and general methods place the flow measurements at 25°C. Salt levels are 0.5 and 1.0 M NaCl, 1.0 M KCl, 1.0 M CaCl₂; sucrose levels are 10/20/30% w/v.[3] The corrected article does not turn these into suggested recipe levels, does not call sucrose concentration total soluble solids or °Brix, and does not infer pH×salt×sugar factorial testing.

The pH 1–10 wording is the authors' description across discrete test points. The earlier contradictory pH response remains attributed to the 2019 paper; the prior Alpizar-Reyes paper was not independently read. This is not a two-study independent replication claim.[3]

### 5. Emulsification and PGA

Independently reopened the archived two-page PDF, re-extracted both pages and visually inspected rendered images of both pages. The precise promotional wording is “Glyloid acts as a natural emulsifier and can help stabilize sauces and dressings without the need for ingredients such as PGA.” The bulletin supplies photographs and general benefits, not a matched formula, oil loading, homogenization protocol, droplet-size results or replacement ratio.[6]

The absence conclusion is restricted to this two-page bulletin, not all supplier documentation. Decorative food photographs are not experimental data. The article neither adopts the bulletin's separate cost, freeze-thaw, allergen-free or shelf-life claims nor implies that absence of published data proves the application cannot work.[6]
