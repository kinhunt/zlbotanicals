# Olive leaf extract in acidic drinks: when liposomes help—and when they do not

A more elaborate delivery system is not automatically a more stable ingredient. In one olive-leaf study, liposomes improved oleuropein retention in a simple acidic model drink, but the unencapsulated extract performed better in a commercial lemonade under the same refrigerated storage period.[4] That reversal is useful for beverage developers: before paying for a carrier, establish what the drink itself does to the compound you want to retain.

The practical question is not simply whether olive leaf extract dissolves. It is whether a chosen material can deliver an acceptable taste while preserving the intended phenolic composition through processing and storage. Bitterness, oleuropein concentration and an overall antioxidant assay answer different questions; treating one as a substitute for the others can lead to the wrong ingredient decision.

## A powder specification does not describe the liquid you will sell

Consider a real purchasing document. Nutraceuticals Group's April 2025 master specification for NIGEHER028501 describes a brownish-greenish fine olive-leaf powder, an indicative 4:1 extract ratio and an assay of at least 6% oleuropein.[5] Its odour and taste description is “Characteristic.”[5] The stated two-year shelf life is conditional on its storage instructions: a clean, cool, dry place below 25°C, with relative humidity below 60% and protection from sunlight.[5]

That document is useful for identifying a dry raw material. It does not establish bitterness at your serving concentration, dissolution in a finished drink or two years of liquid stability. In particular, “4:1” and “6% oleuropein” are different specification fields, not interchangeable descriptions of strength.[5]

The material used in the liposome study was different: an olive-leaf extract supplied by Oleafit S.r.l., Italy, obtained by maceration in 70% aqueous ethanol and specified at a minimum of 40 wt% oleuropein on a dry-weight basis.[4] The paper is not an application test of the Nutraceuticals Group powder. The comparison matters because “olive leaf extract” alone is not enough information to transfer a result between grades.

Even the study's free-extract pH experiment was not a simple stir-in-water test. The researchers first dissolved extract in ethanol, added buffer, centrifuged out insoluble material and adjusted the final volume.[4] A stable marker in that prepared liquid is not proof that a different powder will produce a clear beverage without the same preparation.

## Define retention before trying to soften bitterness

For a product designed around oleuropein, the relevant question is how much oleuropein remains—not just whether the liquid still returns a substantial total-phenol or radical-scavenging result.

A separate heat-treatment study examined aqueous olive-leaf extract over pH 2.0–6.0 and temperatures of 5–90°C.[7] Its abstract reports faster oleuropein degradation at pH 2.0, increasing hydroxytyrosol during hydrolysis, but relatively little change in total phenolic content and ABTS radical-scavenging activity.[7] The reported endpoint was chemical composition and reducing capacity, not finished-drink efficacy.

The formulation implication is specific: conversion of one phenolic compound into a different mixture can leave an aggregate assay looking comparatively steady. If the brief is to retain oleuropein, a stable total-phenol result does not settle the question. Conversely, if the aim is a hydroxytyrosol-led material, that is a different composition target, not successful preservation of the original oleuropein specification.

Taste needs its own evidence. The abstract of a fruit-smoothie study reports that a trained panel of 11 assessed the bitterness of olive-leaf-extract-fortified smoothies.[8] The reported detection and recognition thresholds were 5.78 and 8.05 mg of OLE polyphenols per 100 g of smoothie—not milligrams of extract powder.[8] At 20 mg polyphenols per 100 g, sucrose reduced perceived bitterness by 24.9%, while sodium chloride did not mask it effectively in that experiment.[8]

The thresholds apply to the tested smoothies, not automatically to clear drinks; the abstract does not give a sucrose concentration to use in a new recipe.[8] Sensory masking and oleuropein retention therefore need separate tests.

## What refrigeration and pH actually showed

In a separate experiment within the Food Biophysics study, free extract in buffers was stored at 5°C.[4] After 35 days, the researchers reported approximately 62% oleuropein remaining at pH 2.0, versus approximately 98% at pH 4.0 and 92% at pH 6.0.[4][9] At pH 7.0, retention was approximately 67%.[4][9]

This is more informative than saying “acid is bad” or “neutral is safe.” Under this experiment's conditions, both the strongly acidic and neutral systems lost more oleuropein than the intermediate-pH systems. The buffers also differed, so these values describe the tested preparations rather than a universal pH specification.[4]

For a developer, the result is a reason to compare realistic versions of the intended beverage—not a reason to move every recipe to pH 4 or 6. Selecting a beverage's pH also requires its own food-safety and process design; these chemical-retention results do not validate either.

## The revealing result: the drink changed the winner

The researchers made olive-leaf liposomes using Phospholipon 90 g, a soybean phosphatidylcholine material from Phospholipid GmbH.[4] Oleuropein encapsulation efficiency was about 34%: the measured oleuropein mass in the separated liposome fraction divided by the measured total in that fraction plus the nonencapsulated supernatant.[4] The denominator was oleuropein, not extract powder or carrier mass. Roughly two-thirds of the measured oleuropein was therefore in the nonencapsulated fraction; the later drink results measured total oleuropein, not just the encapsulated portion.[4]

They then compared free extract and the liposomal preparation in three liquid systems: a commercial Sola limonada lemonade, a pH-matched citrate model containing ascorbic acid, and the same model without ascorbic acid.[4] The model pH was 2.87; the ascorbic-acid model contained 1.2 mg/mL ascorbic acid.[4] The final oleuropein loading was approximately 130 µg/mL, and all drink samples were stored at 5°C for 47 days.[4] The two preparations were compared at matched total oleuropein loading.[4]

The results did not support one winner across all three liquids:

- **Simple acid model, without ascorbic acid:** approximately 67% oleuropein remained with free extract, compared with 88% in the liposomal preparation.[4]
- **Acid model with ascorbic acid:** losses were only about 10–13%, with no statistically significant difference reported between the free and liposomal preparations.[4]
- **Commercial lemonade:** approximately 90% remained with free extract, versus 83% with the liposomal preparation; the authors reported significantly better stability for the free-extract addition.[4]

The useful finding is the change in ranking, not a promise that adding vitamin C will fix every olive-leaf drink. The authors proposed a protective role for ascorbic acid but explicitly called for more work to understand the mechanism.[4] Their work supports screening the actual beverage matrix before choosing encapsulation; it does not establish an ambient shelf life, a production heat treatment or sensory acceptability for the experimental drinks.

## Turn the evidence into an ingredient decision

Start with the product's intended identity. If the brand wants an oleuropein-led olive-leaf drink, write that into the development brief. Specify an individual-compound endpoint alongside any total-polyphenol measurement. If a less bitter, hydrolysed ingredient is being considered instead, treat it as a material-selection change, with a new composition and taste target—not a minor flavour adjustment.

Next, give the free extract a fair comparison in the complete proposed drink. The study's commercial lemonade result makes this more than a cost-saving intuition: in that particular refrigerated matrix, a carrier did not improve the measured endpoint.[4] A carrier trial should therefore answer a defined problem that the simpler preparation has failed to solve.

When comparing the two routes, hold the initial oleuropein loading constant rather than comparing equal weights of unlike ingredient preparations. Distinguish total oleuropein in the drink from the amount actually encapsulated. The roughly 34% efficiency in the published experiment illustrates why a carrier's name alone is not a performance specification.[4]

Finally, decide whether a formulation is succeeding on both axes: retained composition and acceptable flavour. A sweeter drink with declining oleuropein is not the same outcome as a stable oleuropein drink with successful bitterness masking. Nor does a preserved marker compensate for an unacceptable taste.

For sourcing, the most useful opening question is therefore: **“Can this specific olive-leaf material meet our taste and oleuropein-retention targets in this specific liquid, without a carrier—and what evidence would justify adding one?”** That is a stronger starting point than purchasing the highest assay or the most elaborate delivery system first.
