# Astaxanthin drinks: choosing interfaces for liquid and powder formats

For an astaxanthin beverage, the coating around an oil droplet affects both processing and performance in the drink. Research with *Haematococcus pluvialis* oleoresin illustrates the tradeoff: an interface that protects astaxanthin during spray-drying may respond poorly when the drink's pH changes.[2]

Liquid concentrates and dry beverage mixes therefore need different development routes. A liquid dispersion must maintain the desired appearance through processing and storage; a powder must also withstand drying and redisperse during preparation. Three experimental studies offer useful starting points for choosing the interface and manufacturing sequence.

## A dispersed orange-red color, rather than a direct shade replacement

A 2020 study incorporated an emulsion made from *H. pluvialis* oleoresin into a commercial colorless isotonic beverage. The researchers mixed water, propylene glycol, sugar and a sucrose monoester surfactant, homogenized the mixture, added the oleoresin, and then applied further mixing and ultrasound.[1] The resulting ingredient dispersed in the beverage; the experiment did not show that the oleoresin itself dissolved in water.

The prototypes produced an orange-red appearance. The closest match to the selected red commercial beverage still had a reported color difference, ΔE, of 8.05 ± 0.09, which the authors considered visible.[1] This makes the work more relevant to developing a new shade than to reproducing an existing one exactly. For a new product, the target can be an acceptable orange-red appearance; for reformulation, it must be the existing drink's color tolerance.

The storage test also points to packaging and distribution as early development decisions. Samples exposed to intense light at 30 ± 2 °C lost their color within eight days. Foil-wrapped samples refrigerated at 5 ± 2 °C performed better, although their color also changed during storage.[1] Because light and temperature changed together, the experiment cannot isolate the benefit of a light barrier or establish an ambient shelf life. Candidate packaging and intended storage conditions belong in the color trials from the start.

## Two layers retained as much astaxanthin as three during drying

A 2021 study combined algal astaxanthin oleoresin with sunflower oil and compared three oil-droplet interfaces: lupin protein alone; lupin protein followed by ι-carrageenan; and that two-layer structure followed by chitosan. Before spray-drying, the researchers added maltodextrin to bring each feed to 15% total solids.[2]

### Reported astaxanthin retention immediately after spray-drying

| Oil-droplet interface | 120 °C inlet air | 160 °C inlet air |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Lupin protein | 88.5% | 87.4% |
| Lupin protein + ι-carrageenan | 96.4% | 95.3% |
| Lupin protein + ι-carrageenan + chitosan | 96.8% | 96.0% |

Source: [2], sections 2.10–2.11 and 3.5. Retention is measured astaxanthin content after drying divided by the study's theoretical content in the powder, multiplied by 100. The assay used solvent extraction and UV–visible absorbance at 478 nm. Temperatures are dryer inlet air temperatures, not measured particle temperatures.

Both multilayer systems had significantly higher reported retention than the protein-only system; the difference between two and three layers was not statistically significant.[2] Layer construction also changed the feed composition and dilution, so this is a comparison of the tested formulations rather than an isolated effect of layer count. The values describe the immediate drying endpoint, not powder shelf life or performance after reconstitution.

The two-layer system also had more favorable pH behavior in the liquid-emulsion tests. After 24 hours at ambient temperature, it remained negatively charged without reported phase separation across the tested pH range of 3–7. The three-layer emulsion showed declining positive charge and aggregation above pH 4.3.[2] This short pH test should be read separately from the study's seven-day storage test, in which both multilayer emulsions showed some creaming.[2]

For a dry beverage mix, these results give a concrete reason to evaluate the protein–carrageenan route before adding a chitosan step. The next application test is reconstitution in the intended drink: whether the powder disperses evenly, produces the desired color and remains uniform over the expected time between preparation and consumption.

## Premixing changes the route, but composition matters too

A 2024 model-emulsion study compared two ways to combine sodium caseinate and sugar beet pectin. One formed a protein-stabilized emulsion first and then added pectin; the other premixed the protein and pectin before emulsification.[3]

The selected premixed formulation performed better in several acidity and salt tests. For example, the layered emulsion showed pronounced droplet flocculation at pH 3, while the premixed emulsion showed no notable microstructural change across the tested pH values.[3] But the two routes used different protein-to-pectin ratios and final biopolymer concentrations, as recorded in the paper's Table 1.[3] A development comparison should therefore vary composition alongside addition order, rather than assume that premixing alone produces the improvement.

The paper also shows why a fresh emulsion's appearance is not enough to select a ready-to-drink system. Both astaxanthin-loaded formulations separated after one day of storage under room-temperature and light conditions.[3] A useful follow-up would track separation as well as color and astaxanthin content, instead of choosing a route only from its initial droplet properties.

This was a laboratory model: the stock solutions contained sodium azide to suppress microbial growth, and the purchased astaxanthin's algal origin and ester profile were not specified.[3] The study informs process design; its formulation is not an edible recipe or a direct validation of algal oleoresin.

## Match the development route to the finished drink

For a **ready-to-drink beverage**, begin with the final liquid matrix and its intended package. The isotonic-beverage experiment gives an orange-red prototype to build on, while the caseinate–pectin experiment identifies addition order and composition as variables worth comparing.[1][3] Screen candidate dispersions at the drink's actual pH and mineral composition, then carry the promising samples through the intended process and storage conditions.

For a **dry beverage mix**, connect the dryer trial to preparation of a serving. The lupin–carrageenan system is a reasonable experimental starting point because the third layer brought no significant additional drying-retention benefit in that study.[2] Reconstitute candidate powders at the intended serving concentration and compare uniformity and appearance over the expected consumption period. This tests the part of the application that the reported drying-retention values do not cover.

Cost comparisons should include the steps needed to reach the finished product: interface preparation, homogenization, drying where applicable, and acceptable performance in the drink. An extra coating step is useful when it solves a measured application problem, not simply because it adds another layer.

Before commercial development, check ingredient eligibility, permitted uses and label claims for the destination market. These formulation studies establish neither market authorization nor clinical benefits.
