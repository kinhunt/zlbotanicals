# Astaxanthin drinks: design the oil-droplet interface before choosing the powder

An astaxanthin drink project needs an early decision that an assay percentage cannot make: will the ingredient travel through the supply chain as an emulsion concentrate, a dry powder, or a finished liquid? These are different development routes, not simply different pack sizes. Research with *Haematococcus pluvialis* oleoresin shows why: a coating that helps retain astaxanthin through spray-drying is not automatically the best coating across a drink's pH range.[2]

For a beverage team, the useful question is therefore not “Which form has the highest content?” It is “Where must this ingredient work—in the dryer, during reconstitution, or throughout liquid storage?” The answer should determine the application brief before samples are compared.

## A beverage route exists, but it is not just oleoresin plus water

A 2020 study incorporated an emulsion made from *H. pluvialis* oleoresin into a commercial colorless isotonic beverage. The researchers first made an emulsion using water, propylene glycol, sugar and a sucrose monoester surfactant, followed by high-speed homogenization and ultrasound treatment.[1] This was an engineered dispersion, not a demonstration that the oleoresin itself dissolved in the drink.

The work also illustrates a commercial distinction between a new color direction and a like-for-like color replacement. Of the prototypes tested, the closest match to the chosen red commercial beverage still had a reported color difference, ΔE, of 8.05 ± 0.09; the authors considered that difference visible.[1] A brand developing its own orange-red appearance has a different brief from one required to reproduce an existing shade. “Natural replacement” should not silently mean “visually identical.”

Packaging belongs in that brief too. The study compared illuminated samples held at 30 ± 2 °C with foil-wrapped samples refrigerated at 5 ± 2 °C.[1] The illuminated samples lost their color within eight days, while the protected, refrigerated samples performed better.[1] Because light and temperature changed together, this experiment does not isolate the benefit of a light barrier or establish an ambient shelf life. Its practical value is to put the intended pack and distribution conditions into development from the beginning, rather than after a color has been approved at the bench.

## For a powder, more coating is not always more useful

A separate 2021 study used algal astaxanthin oleoresin in sunflower oil and built three oil-droplet interfaces: lupin protein alone; lupin protein followed by ι-carrageenan; and that two-layer structure followed by chitosan.[2] Maltodextrin was then added before spray-drying.[2]

The second layer made a meaningful difference at the dryer. At inlet air temperatures of 120 and 160 °C, the reported astaxanthin retention was 88.5% and 87.4% for the protein-only system, versus 96.4% and 95.3% for the protein–carrageenan system.[2] Adding the third, chitosan layer produced 96.8% and 96.0%, with no statistically significant retention difference between the two- and three-layer systems.[2] These were immediate post-drying measurements, not results after months of powder storage or after reconstitution.[2]

The third layer also changed the pH response. The two-layer emulsion remained negatively charged without phase separation over the tested pH range of 3–7, whereas the three-layer emulsion lost charge and aggregated above pH 4.3.[2] In this experiment, adding another protective layer did not deliver a correspondingly better processing result and narrowed the useful pH behavior.

That is a useful development decision, not an argument against encapsulation. If the intended product is a dry beverage mix, the two-layer route merits evaluation before a more elaborate coating is commissioned. The powder still has to redisperse in the actual drink. A high retention figure at the dryer exit cannot answer how a consumer-reconstituted serving will look or behave.

## The manufacturing sequence can change the result

A 2024 model-emulsion study offers another route: combine the protein and polysaccharide before emulsification instead of assembling layers on an existing oil droplet. It compared sodium caseinate–sugar beet pectin systems made by sequential layering with systems made from premixed biopolymers.[3]

The selected premixed system tolerated acidity and salts better in several tests, but the comparison was not a clean test of addition order alone. The researchers selected different protein-to-pectin ratios for the two routes, and Table 1 shows different final biopolymer concentrations.[3] This supports investigating process and composition together; it does not establish that premixing is universally superior.

There is a further reason not to turn this paper into a ready-made beverage recipe. Its laboratory stock solutions contained sodium azide to inhibit microbial growth, and its methods did not establish an algal origin or ester profile for the purchased astaxanthin.[3] It is supporting evidence about interface design, not a food formulation to copy or a direct validation of a particular algal oleoresin.

Even the better-performing route had limits: the astaxanthin-loaded emulsions showed phase separation after one day of storage.[3] Better comparative performance is a lead for development, not a finished ready-to-drink system.

## Buy a development route, not an isolated retention number

The commercial implication is to separate two sample briefs.

For a **ready-to-drink beverage**, ask for a dispersion developed around the final drink and its package. The central decision is whether the proposed interface survives the actual acid, mineral, processing and distribution conditions while giving the desired appearance. The isotonic-beverage study makes a new orange-red concept plausible, but does not establish an exact replacement shade or an ambient shelf-life claim.[1]

For a **dry beverage mix**, ask for evidence that connects powder manufacture to reconstitution. The lupin–carrageenan study gives a concrete reason to investigate a two-layer interface, but its high retention values stop at the immediate post-drying endpoint.[2] Reconstituted appearance and stability remain separate development work.

These briefs should be compared on the cost and complexity of reaching the intended finished product—not just on raw-material price or a percentage on a sample label. A simpler coating that meets the application may be a better starting point than an additional layer with no demonstrated benefit for that application.

The most useful next step is a project brief stating the liquid or powder route, target appearance, final pH and mineral composition, processing sequence, packaging and distribution conditions. That enables a relevant sample discussion without pretending that one published emulsion is already a commercial formula. Ingredient eligibility, permitted uses and label claims must then be checked for the destination market; none of these studies establishes universal authorization.
