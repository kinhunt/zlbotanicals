# Cocoa powder or cocoa extract? Match chocolate character and flavanol delivery separately

A chocolate drink and a compact flavanol supplement may start with the same plant, but they are not buying the same ingredient function. One needs a cocoa matrix with the right colour, flavour and suspended solids; the other needs a defined amount of cocoa flavanols within a small serving. An unusually useful comparison appears in a 2015 Food Safety Authority of Ireland opinion: Mars’ defatted cocoa cake was either milled into powder or subjected to an additional extraction step. The resulting extract contained relatively more flavanols, caffeine and theobromine, and less fat, protein and dietary fibre than its powder comparator.[4]

That does not make every extract a better cocoa powder. It means the formulation has changed. For buyers, the useful starting point is three-way selection: conventional cocoa powder, flavanol-preserving cocoa powder, or a composition-defined cocoa extract.

These are named public examples, not claims about another supplier’s stock or certifications. The FSAI material is a historical dossier; the deZaan PDF contains a 2024 technical specification and 2025 indicative nutrition information.[1][4]

## Why “10/12 cocoa” is not enough for substitution

D-11-A and S8 both list 10–12% fat, yet their stated pH ranges are 7.0–7.4 and 7.9–8.3 respectively.[1][2] That is a material distinction for a developer transferring a chocolate drink, dessert or bakery formulation. The purchasing shorthand captures fat, not the whole colour and processing profile.

D-11-A’s technical sheet also demonstrates the difference between fine powder and soluble extract. Its fineness result is a sieve specification, measured using a water suspension; its indicative composition includes 26.5 g insoluble fibre per 100 g.[1] Passing a fine sieve does not make that fibre dissolve. A manufacturer seeking an opaque cocoa drink should therefore compare wetting, sediment and mouthfeel in the actual drink, rather than reject every sedimenting sample as chemically defective.

For a powder-to-extract substitution, the problem is larger. Reducing cocoa matrix solids may reduce the amount of ingredient required for a flavanol target, but it also changes what is available to build body and chocolate character. That is a reason to run a formulation comparison, not to assume a one-for-one replacement.

## Darker cocoa is not a flavanol specification

Miller and colleagues’ 2008 commercial-powder survey reported mean total flavanols of 34.6 ± 6.8 mg/g for natural cocoa, 13.8 ± 7.3 mg/g for lightly processed cocoa, 7.8 ± 4.0 mg/g for medium-processed cocoa and 3.9 ± 1.8 mg/g for heavily processed cocoa. Flavanol content decreased as extractable pH increased in those surveyed materials.[5]

This is a historical analytical survey, not a guaranteed assay for a current supplier grade. It also does not mean alkalized powder contains zero flavanols. The current Acticoa SKU description combines preserved-flavanol positioning with a light-alkalized pH category.[3] Processing category is informative, but it cannot replace the selected material’s actual flavanol result.

The practical consequence is to keep two development targets separate. Choose colour and flavour against a sensory reference; choose flavanol delivery against a defined analytical result. A powder can perform well on one axis and poorly on the other. Neither “dark” nor “natural” supplies the missing assay.

## Flavanol concentration does not remove caffeine or theobromine

In the FSAI comparison, extraction increased the relative content of caffeine and theobromine as well as flavanols.[4] A concentrated cocoa ingredient therefore cannot be assumed to be decaffeinated. The denominator matters: a higher concentration per gram can still produce a different daily intake if the serving becomes smaller.

The D-11-A indicative nutrition sheet lists theobromine at 2.1 g/100 g and caffeine at 0.2 g/100 g.[1] At an illustrative 5 g addition, that corresponds to approximately 105 mg theobromine and 10 mg caffeine. This calculation uses indicative composition, not a batch release result or a recommended serving. It is useful for early formula screening; the final exposure calculation needs the actual selected material and maximum daily servings.

The FSAI dossier described a proposed serving of up to 730 mg extract providing 375 mg cocoa flavanols.[4] Those paired amounts imply about 51.4% flavanols for that dossier’s serving example. They are not a generic commercial specification and should not be assigned to an unrelated “cocoa extract 10:1.” An extraction ratio alone gives neither flavanol content nor methylxanthine exposure.

## Two useful development routes

### Chocolate drink: design the cocoa experience first

For an opaque dairy or plant-based cocoa drink, start with the colour, flavour and texture benchmark. Use a defined powder grade as the cocoa matrix. If flavanol delivery is also a product target, compare a flavanol-preserving powder with a combination of flavour-led powder and an assayed extract. Acticoa’s public positioning shows why this middle category belongs in the comparison.[3]

A useful pilot design is to preblend the cocoa powder with part of the dry base, wet it into a controlled portion of liquid, and compare dispersion before and after the intended homogenization and heat treatment. If the recipe uses a suspension stabilizer, hydrate it by its own validated procedure rather than assume a higher dose will fix poor wetting. These are proposed development steps, not a validated supplier recipe.

Judge the complete drink: cocoa intensity, bitter/astringent finish, sediment, redispersibility, viscosity and retained flavanols. Adding a concentrated extract should trigger a new sensory and analytical comparison, not merely a change to the ingredient cost sheet. Neither an extract name nor a small bench sample establishes shelf-stable clarity.

### Compact supplement: design around measured composition

For a capsule or tablet brief, start with the required ingredient mass and the measured flavanol, caffeine and theobromine contents. The historical Mars dossier illustrates why extraction can move all three together.[4] There is no reason to import cocoa-powder colour or fat shorthand as the principal purchase criterion for this task.

Trial a geometrically diluted premix with the intended excipients, then assess blend and unit uniformity, flow, filling or compression behaviour, disintegration and stability. These are development recommendations. A high flavanol assay alone does not establish that a powder will fill consistently or that a tablet will disintegrate appropriately.

## Compare cost per accepted formulation, not just per kilogram

For an early material screen, calculate ingredient mass as target flavanols in milligrams divided by the measured milligrams of flavanols per gram. This comparison is valid only when the assays use a comparable definition and the same supplied-material basis. A total-polyphenol declaration should not be inserted into the calculation as though it were the same measurand as cocoa flavanols.

Then add the application cost: the powder needed for chocolate character, any extra matrix ingredients needed after substitution, process changes and the fraction of pilot formulas that meet the sensory and stability brief. An extract with a higher unit price might require less mass; it might also fail the flavour brief. No price advantage follows from concentration alone.

The best purchase description consequently names the function: **cocoa powder for a defined chocolate matrix**, **flavanol-preserving powder for combined sensory and composition targets**, or **assayed cocoa extract for controlled flavanol delivery**. This is more useful than treating “cocoa” as a single interchangeable botanical ingredient.

## Evidence scope

Supplier documents support the named composition and product-positioning examples; the Miller source was read as an abstract, not full experimental methods. The FSAI opinion concerns a particular 2015 dossier and its proposed uses, not blanket present-day market authorization. No disease-prevention claim, clinical dose recommendation, current inventory or finished-formula validation is made here.
