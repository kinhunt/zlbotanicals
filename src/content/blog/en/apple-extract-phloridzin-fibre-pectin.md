An “apple ingredient” quotation can describe a polyphenol mixture, a material specified for phloridzin, a water-binding fruit fibre or an isolated pectin. These are different purchasing categories, even when they begin with the same fruit or juice-processing residue. A high polyphenol result does not establish fibre performance; a fibre percentage does not specify a phloridzin dose.

The practical starting point is the ingredient's job. Is the project seeking a reproducible phenolic profile, a named molecular marker, water retention and body, or a pectin system with a defined setting behaviour? That decision determines which samples, analytical results and application tests belong in the comparison.

## What two manufacturers actually describe

BGG identifies ApplePhenon® as an extract of selected immature green apples, with the origin named as *Malus pumila* Mill. Its product page lists <strong>“60% procyanidins powder (UV)”</strong>. That is a procyanidin specification with a stated analytical technique; it is not a 60% phloridzin declaration. The page does not provide enough procedural detail to reproduce the UV assay from that line alone. A buyer comparing it with another offer should request the method, reference material and reporting basis rather than silently replace “procyanidins” with “total polyphenols.” <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-1">[1]</a>

Herbafood's Herbacel® AQ® AFB-200 and AFB-250 page addresses a different job: functional <strong>apple fibres</strong> with water-binding and swelling properties at cold and warm temperatures. It distinguishes AFB-200, which supports a golden-brown colour, from AFB-250, intended for applications where a bright or colour-neutral appearance matters. Both are positioned for use alongside starches, citrus fibre and gums. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-2">[2]</a>

These supplier descriptions make the commercial distinction tangible. The extract is presented through a phenolic constituent specification; the fibres are differentiated through hydration, texture and colour. The fibre page labels its water-binding comparison “Internal methode no. 193.” Without the full procedure, its graphical comparison should not become a universal water-binding figure for all apple fibres. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-2">[2]</a>

For a pale filling, dairy alternative or light-coloured bakery product, the fibre's colour may be an early exclusion criterion. For a phenolic ingredient, the first exclusion criterion may instead be the wrong plant part, an unspecified carrier or an assay that measures a different constituent. Neither choice is resolved by ranking headline percentages.

## One pomace stream can produce separate polyphenol and pectin fractions

Yilmaz-Turan and colleagues studied a sequential process using wet pressed apple pomace supplied by a Swedish juice producer. They first milled the wet material and extracted it with <strong>50% v/v ethanol</strong>, at a reported <strong>1:10 pomace-to-solvent ratio</strong>, stirring for <strong>one hour at room temperature in the dark</strong>. The concentrated phenolic fraction was freeze-dried. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-3">[3, §2.2.1]</a>

They then used the remaining pomace for subcritical-water extraction of pectic polysaccharides, testing <strong>120°C and 140°C</strong>, with <strong>pH 3.0, 5.0 and 7.0</strong> and sequential extraction periods. The recovered pectin fractions were dialysed and freeze-dried. These were laboratory-separated materials, not one powder carrying two interchangeable names. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-3">[3, §2.2.2]</a>

The analyses also followed different targets. Phenolic recovery used a Folin–Ciocalteau measurement calibrated with gallic acid, alongside phenolic-acid chromatography. Pectin characterization examined sugar composition, molecular-size distribution and methylation, among other properties. Extraction conditions changed the pectin populations recovered. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-3">[3, §§2.3, 3.3–3.5]</a>

This study provides a useful sourcing distinction: <strong>“made from apple pomace” describes a starting material, not the identity of the resulting ingredient</strong>. Ask which fraction is collected, which soluble or insoluble components are removed, and what remains in the powder. A phenolic extract should not inherit the water-binding specification of the residual fibre; a pectin ingredient should not inherit the phenolic assay of the first extraction stream.

The paper's laboratory radical-scavenging test is likewise not a finished-food shelf-life test. If an extract is being considered to control oxidation in a formula, compare it against the appropriate control in that food, using its actual processing and storage conditions. That is a separate development question from measuring the incoming extract's phenolic content.

## Phloridzin is a marker, not another name for apple polyphenols

Phloridzin is a dihydrochalcone glycoside: phloretin linked to a glucose moiety. Phloretin is the corresponding aglycone. In a primary analytical study, Adamcová and colleagues separated and measured phloridzin and phloretin as different analytes, alongside chlorogenic acid, rutin and quercitrin. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-4">[4]</a>

Their materials were <strong>apple-tree leaves, bark and buds</strong>, not fruit extract. For example, Table 3 reports leaf phloridzin values of <strong>46.43 mg/g dry weight</strong> for ‘Goldstar’ and <strong>98.51 mg/g dry weight</strong> for ‘Spartan’. Those denominators describe the dried plant material analysed, not the purity of a commercially isolated powder. The method used methanolic laboratory extraction followed by HPLC-DAD. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-4">[4, Table 3; §3]</a>

The procurement consequence is specific: a request for “apple phloridzin” needs a plant-part declaration. A leaf or bark study cannot be used to fill in a missing fruit-extract specification. Equally, identifying a compound in a plant part does not establish that part's suitability for a particular food category or market.

For a marker-defined ingredient, ask for chromatographic separation and quantification of phloridzin, with phloretin separately identified where relevant. Record the reference standard, sample preparation, reporting basis and carrier content. A mixed extract can contain phloridzin without being a phloridzin concentrate; a purified or enriched ingredient may no longer represent the full phenolic mixture of the starting fruit.

## A pomace chromatogram shows why “percent” needs a denominator

Orozco-Flores and colleagues analysed *Malus domestica* Golden Delicious pomace from a juice-processing facility in Chihuahua, Mexico. Their work included ethanol–water extraction assisted by an ultrasonic bath and chromatographic profiling. The selected solvent system is reported as <strong>80–20% ethanol–water/acetic acid 5% (S1)</strong>. These conditions describe the study preparation, not a recommended manufacturing recipe. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-5">[5, Materials and Methods]</a>

These are <strong>peak-area percentages</strong>, not mass percentages in pomace or in a commercial dry extract. The paper describes its profile assignments as tentative and uses available standards and mass-spectrometric information in its identification work. The area values therefore must not be recast as a specification for purified phloridzin or used to calculate a serving dose. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-5">[5, Table 3]</a>

A separate result in Table 2 is the phenolic extract's HPLC total, reported as <strong>0.56 ± 0.01 g per 100 g of apple pomace</strong>; the results text identifies the basis as fresh weight. The paper expresses the chromatographic total in <strong>epicatechin equivalents</strong>. This is neither 0.56 g phloridzin nor a 0.56% purity result for dried commercial extract. <a href="#apple-extract-phloridzin-fibre-pectin-ref-5">[5, Table 2; Methods and Results]</a>

Three reporting questions prevent that error:

- <strong>What is counted?</strong> A named molecule, a method-defined phenolic total or chromatographic area?
- <strong>What is it calibrated against?</strong> Phloridzin standard, epicatechin equivalents, gallic acid equivalents or another reference?
- <strong>What is the denominator?</strong> Fresh pomace, dried plant material, dry extract or powder as supplied?

For example, converting 0.56 g per 100 g to <strong>5.6 mg per g</strong> changes the unit but does not change the fresh-pomace denominator or the epicatechin-equivalent basis. It still cannot be compared directly with BGG's UV procyanidin percentage. There is no universal conversion factor between these different analytical results.

## Choose the trial that answers the ingredient's job

For a <strong>polyphenol beverage</strong>, start with a material-identity and composition screen, then compare candidates at a clearly defined powder addition and, where methods genuinely match, a defined phenolic or marker loading. Evaluate dispersibility, colour, astringency, sediment and the relevant analyte after the actual heat process and storage. Reporting both powder addition and marker loading helps reveal carrier or concentration differences instead of hiding them.

For <strong>apple fibre in a filling or bakery formula</strong>, compare hydration under the planned mixing conditions, water release, texture and colour. The AFB-200/AFB-250 distinction shows why two apple fibres may have different visual suitability even before their texture is assessed. Keep total water and the addition sequence controlled in the initial trial; otherwise a water adjustment can be mistaken for a superior fibre. These are proposed formulation checks, not results already obtained for the supplier grades.

For a <strong>pectin-led gel or protein-stabilisation task</strong>, choose a pectin grade and evaluate its own process window. Apple origin alone does not specify gelation or calcium response. The separate [pectin selection guide](/resources/blog/citrus-pectin-gel-selection) covers the setting-window and application-grade questions in more detail.

If a formulation uses polyphenol extract and fibre together, qualify them separately before evaluating the blend. Their coexistence in a fruit does not determine the optimum ratio, physical stability or acceptable taste in a manufactured product.

## A useful apple-ingredient inquiry

> Please identify the apple species and plant part, and state whether the proposed material is a mixed polyphenol extract, a phloridzin-specified ingredient, functional apple fibre or pectin. Provide the processing route, carrier or standardisation ingredients, and results on a clearly stated as-supplied or dry basis. For the phenolic option, include the assay procedure, reference standard and relevant chromatographic profile. For the fibre or pectin option, include the grade-specific functional test procedure and suggested application conditions.

Attach the intended product, target appearance, processing temperatures, mixing sequence and the performance being replaced or added. This turns “apple extract” from a broad catalogue term into a sample brief that the laboratory, formulator and purchasing team can all use.
