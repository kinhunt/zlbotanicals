Choosing a flaxseed ingredient involves more than deciding how much oil, fibre or lignan it should contain. A full-fat meal that has passed through an extruder and a lignan-rich extract recovered with aqueous ethanol are not simply two strengths of the same material. In published experiments, extrusion lowered a cyanogenic assay result, while ethanolic extraction concentrated cyanogenic glycosides alongside the desired phenolics.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>

For a developer comparing these materials, the useful question is not “Which process removes the most cyanide?” It is: **What was measured, in which material, and what else changed before the ingredient reached its final form?**

## First separate the glycosides from the HCN result

Flaxseed contains cyanogenic glycosides, including linustatin and neolinustatin. Disrupting the seed can bring them into contact with enzymes that release hydrogen cyanide, also called hydrocyanic acid or HCN.<a href="#flax-ref-4">[4]</a><a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> A laboratory can quantify particular glycosides, or hydrolyse them and measure the HCN released under the analytical conditions; those are different ways of describing the material.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a><a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>

The distinction matters when reading a specification:

- **Individual glycosides** identify the compounds included in an analytical panel. The extraction study discussed below measured four glycosides by gas chromatography.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>
- **HCN equivalent or potential** describes what those compounds could yield under the stated calculation or hydrolysis procedure. In that extraction study, the HCN equivalent was calculated from total glycosides; it was not a direct measurement of free HCN already present in the ingredient.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>
- **Released HCN in a defined assay** still needs its method attached. In EFSA's feed-process assessment, extraction and enzymatic hydrolysis preceded distillation and titration of the released HCN.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a>

Do not add glycoside mass to HCN mass, or treat a low free-HCN result as proof that the remaining glycosides have disappeared. Ask the laboratory which endpoint its reported number represents.

## Full-fat meal: a lower cyanogenic result can come with a quality trade-off

A 2015 experiment used the Chandni flaxseed variety and a single-screw industrial extruder. The researchers varied barrel exit temperature, screw speed, feed rate and feed moisture, changing one factor while holding the others at their middle settings.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a> This is not equivalent to warming a packet of meal in a domestic oven.

The untreated material was reported at 198.4 ± 0.6 mg/kg hydrocyanic acid. At a barrel exit temperature of 140 °C, Table 1 reports 31.5 ± 0.6 mg/kg under “cyanogenic compounds”—the basis for the authors' approximately 84% reduction.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a> Methods specify alkaline titration, although the paper alternates between “hydrocyanic acid,” “cyanogenic compounds” and “cyanogenic glycosides.” It should therefore not be presented as a compound-by-compound chromatographic demonstration of glycoside removal.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a>

There was still measurable material at that endpoint. The 140 °C result was not the lowest in the whole table: the separate 10% feed-moisture row reports 26.4 ± 0.3 mg/kg.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a> Nor was cyanogenic content the only consideration: at 140 °C, oil yield was 29.8 ± 0.2 g/100 g, versus 32.2 ± 0.4 g/100 g reported for the raw material.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a> The table's α-linolenic acid figures are percentages of total fatty acids, not grams of omega-3 per 100 g of meal.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a>

Storage also qualified the result. Samples were sealed in polypropylene bags and kept at 20–25 °C without light control; the authors reported poorer sensory acceptability for extruded samples after 90 days, particularly under higher temperature and moisture conditions.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a> Their discussion of a 60-day use window is evidence from that experiment, not a transferable commercial shelf-life claim.<a href="#flax-ref-1">[1]</a>

**Material-selection implication:** request the processed meal's residual cyanogenic result together with oil content, relevant oxidation results and storage validation. A percentage reduction alone does not describe the ingredient you will formulate with.

## Lignan-rich extracts: enrichment can move the result in the opposite direction

A separate primary study compared aqueous and 60% aqueous-ethanol extracts prepared from hexane-defatted meals of three Polish flax varieties. The recovered extracts were freeze-dried; this was not a comparison of two retail seed powders.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>

These are ranges of the reported means across the three varieties, not commercial specification limits.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a> The article's abstract prints **mmol/g**, whereas the body and Table 4 use **μmol/g**; the table above follows Table 4, not the abstract.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>

A second denominator is just as important. Table 4 reports that the ethanolic extracts recovered 66.4–69.8% of the starting meal's total cyanogenic glycosides, after expressing the extract result per gram of starting meal.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a> That does **not** mean the dried extract had a lower concentration than the meal: its concentration per gram of extract was much higher.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a> “Some was not recovered” and “the extract is depleted” are not interchangeable statements.

The aqueous route gave lower glycoside concentrations but also lower antioxidant activity in the assays used.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a> This is a trade-off, not evidence that one solvent is universally better. The authors also discussed low-use-level food applications for ethanolic extracts; that discussion is not a current safety approval for any particular extract or finished product.<a href="#flax-ref-6">[6]</a>

**Material-selection implication:** a lignan or SDG target should be accompanied by a separate cyanogenic specification. Neither a high marker assay nor the solvent name tells you the residual cyanogenic profile of a commercial lot.

## Fermented flaxseed: read the detection limit before “complete removal”

A 2026 kimchi study used flaxseed processed with a defined Lactobacillaceae consortium, followed by degumming and drying, before incorporation into kimchi.<a href="#flax-ref-2">[2]</a> It therefore does not show that simply adding raw flaxseed to a fermented food will achieve the same result.

Its Table 2 reports linustatin and neolinustatin as not detected, each with a stated detection limit of **500 mg/kg**, alongside a separate total-HCN entry of **<10 mg/kg**.<a href="#flax-ref-2">[2]</a> The methods describe NMR quantification of the glycosides, but do not give an equally explicit separate HCN assay procedure.<a href="#flax-ref-2">[2]</a> The HCN result is an author-reported endpoint, not a value that can be independently reconstructed from the two “ND” entries. “Not detected” at those limits must not be rewritten as zero glycosides.

Other details limit direct transfer: the work describes a 1 kg flaxseed batch and calls for further industrial-scale validation; its abstract says eight weeks of kimchi fermentation, while methods and results describe six weeks.<a href="#flax-ref-2">[2]</a> The addition levels of 0.5%, 1.0% and 2.0% refer to the seasoning mixture, not the weight of the entire kimchi product.<a href="#flax-ref-2">[2]</a> These findings support further process investigation, not a ready-made inclusion recommendation.

## What official assessments add—and what they do not

BfR's communication of 16 June 2026 explains that cooking or baking can reduce cyanide risk through HCN evaporation and inactivation of the enzymes responsible for its release.<a href="#flax-ref-4">[4]</a> Risk reduction through these mechanisms is not the same claim as analytical removal of every glycoside. The communication also discusses cadmium, a separate issue that a cyanogenic-processing result does not resolve.<a href="#flax-ref-4">[4]</a>

EFSA's 2017 assessment gives a useful industrial example: enzymatic release of HCN followed by evaporation. Five paired test batches contained **50–70% raw linseed mixed with wheat bran and sunflower cake**, rather than pure flaxseed meal; their measured HCN started at 86–127 mg/kg.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> Table 1 records four treated batches at <10 mg/kg and one at 11 mg/kg, although the accompanying prose says “below 11.”<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> In a larger series of 178 treated samples with differing linseed proportions, the mean was 12 mg/kg and the maximum 32 mg/kg.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> The small paired series should not be turned into a guarantee that every treated batch is below 11 mg/kg.

EFSA explicitly limited this opinion to linseed for animal feed, not linseed cake, and required final proof of sufficient decontamination through analysis of the finished product.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> It also addressed atmospheric HCN emissions.<a href="#flax-ref-5">[5]</a> This is evidence for controlled industrial processing and output testing—not a human-food authorization or a household detoxification recipe.

## Choose the material first, then qualify the process

For an extruded meal intended for a cereal or bakery development project, evaluate residual cyanogenic content alongside the meal's oil, flavour and storage behaviour. For a lignan-rich extract, keep the marker assay and cyanogenic assay separate, and confirm whether the quoted concentration belongs to the starting meal, the dried extract or the supplied blend. For a fermented material, establish the complete process history and the meaning of every “ND” result before comparing it with a heat-treated grade.

A useful supplier discussion starts with the intended food, material form and target market, then asks for a method-linked result on the final supplied ingredient. The literature supports that disciplined comparison; it does not support one universal processing instruction that makes every flaxseed material safe.
