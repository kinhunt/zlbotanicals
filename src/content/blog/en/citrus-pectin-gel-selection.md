Low-sugar fruit spreads need a gel that remains workable through filling and develops the intended texture on cooling. Acidified milk drinks often need protein particles to stay dispersed while the product remains drinkable. Commercial citrus pectin ranges include grades for both functions.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a>

For a fruit-spread replacement, start with the final pH, calcium system, solids and temperature history. These give a supplier a more useful brief than an LM designation or a target DE alone.

## Chemistry narrows the grade selection

High-methoxyl pectin (HM) has more than 50% of its carboxyl groups methyl-esterified; low-methoxyl pectin (LM) has less than 50%. Amidated LM, also called LMA or LMAP, contains amide groups as well, so its degree of amidation (DA) is specified separately from its degree of methylation or esterification (DM or DE).<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a><a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a>

Conventional HM gelation needs an acidic, high-soluble-solids environment. Calcium-mediated LM gelation permits lower-soluble-solids formulations.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a><a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-12">[12]</a> Within either class, the supplier's application grade provides a more specific starting point.

The following citrus grades appear in [Herbstreith & Fox's *The Specialists for Pectin*](https://www.herbstreith-fox.de/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/AWT_Ue_Specialists_for_pectin_en.pdf), pages 21–23 and 29–30. They are grouped by application and chemistry. The entries are supplier application descriptions, not comparative performance scores or current batch specifications.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a>

All entries come from the supplier brochure.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a> TSS refers to soluble solids in these application descriptions, not measured finished-product sugar content. A dash reproduces the brochure's amidation entry. Preserve the complete grade name when requesting a sample: CF 020-C and the separately listed CF 020 have different application entries.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a>

Classic CF 703 and Amid CF 010-D illustrate the importance of the full specification: their DE ranges overlap, while their calcium-reactivity descriptions and application ranges differ.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a>

[Azelis's listing for GENU® Pectin LM-104 AS](https://explore.azelis.com/en_GB/nz_fh/genu-pectin-lm-104-as) provides another commercial example. It describes an E440 pectin standardised with sucrose, with high calcium reactivity, for low-sugar jams, jellies and yoghurt fruit preparations at 20–40% soluble solids.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-4">[4]</a> For a quotation comparison, establish the standardisation ingredients and whether the recommended dose refers to the supplied powder or its pectin content.

## Similar methylation can produce different setting behaviour

Kastner, Einhorn-Stoll and Drusch compared two commercially available citrus pectins: non-amidated LM with DM 30%, and amidated LM with DM 32% and DA 19%.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> These were two different commercial materials with similar DM, not a matched pair differing only in amidation. Their Table 4 also reports different galacturonan contents.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a>

The experiment used a sugar–acid model. The standard LM preparation contained 6 g pectin, sucrose, citric acid, sodium citrate and calcium chloride dihydrate, and was boiled down to 900 g. The authors report maintaining total solids at 30 wt.% across the systems and preparing gels at least in triplicate. The standard rheological cooling programme ran from 100°C to 10°C at 1 K/min.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> Here, total solids is the paper's reported formulation measure; it is distinct from the supplier table's TSS and from a sugar-content measurement.

Three findings help define a replacement trial.

<strong>Setting time and final texture both changed.</strong> At similar calcium-to-carboxyl ratios, the amidated material reached its main structure-development stage earlier during cooling. Its final response was more elastic; the authors described it as brittle, while describing the non-amidated material as softer and more viscous.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> A spreadable texture therefore needs to be specified independently of an “amidated” designation.

<strong>Acidification affected the two materials differently.</strong> In the acidic range discussed in the paper, decreasing pH made the non-amidated gels more liquid-like and the amidated gels more solid-like. The authors attributed this to a declining contribution from calcium bridges and the additional hydrogen-bond contribution involving amide groups.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> The same acid adjustment may change texture differently after a grade replacement.

<strong>Calcium affected the onset of setting.</strong> In the amidated series, the initial structuring temperature rose across the tested calcium ratios. The critical structuring temperature rose initially, then fell slightly at the highest ratio. At that highest ratio, no clear classical gel point was detected; the authors proposed rapid local microgel formation before measurement as an explanation.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> Early thickening and local particles are therefore useful observations during a process trial, alongside the cooled gel's texture.

The study expressed calcium loading as <strong>R = 2[Ca²⁺]/[COO⁻]</strong>, relating calcium to free carboxyl content.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> Applying it to a commercial powder requires information about that material's composition and available carboxyl groups; it is not a ready-made elemental-calcium dose per serving.

## Calcium tolerance still has a working range

H&F describes amidated LM pectins as forming sufficiently firm gels with small calcium quantities and tolerating a wider calcium-concentration range.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-12">[12]</a> Its general brochure warns that exceeding the calcium optimum can lead to brittle gels, syneresis or insoluble calcium pectinate.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a> It also lists low, medium, high and very high calcium reactivity among different amidated citrus grades.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a> A useful tolerance range must therefore be established for the chosen grade and formulation.

The 2017 paper contains an important table–text discrepancy. In Table 2, increasing R from 0.73 to 1.45 raises the final loss factor from 0.079 to 0.101. The average structure-development rates fluctuate and finish lower, although the discussion says they increased significantly and goes on to describe the gels as more solid-like.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-6">[6]</a> The table does not support those directional claims: it shows a greater relative viscous contribution at higher R, and no consistent rise in the average development rate. This calcium-series result should be kept separate from the direct comparison between the two pectin types.

For development, compare the candidate and incumbent over the calcium and pH range expected in the formula. Keep total solids and the heating/cooling programme fixed in the first comparison. Record the onset of structure, flow during filling, cooled texture and water release. This is a suggested screening design rather than a replication of the paper's recipe.

If a candidate forms particles or loses spreadability at another calcium level, check its reaction range and addition procedure before increasing the pectin dose. Product-specific food-safety, preservation and shelf-life validation remain separate from this texture screening.

## Acidified milk drinks need protein stabilisation

H&F lists Classic CM 203 as a protective colloid for low-viscosity fermented milk drinks, alongside other grades intended for gels and yoghurt texture.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-11">[11]</a> That application distinction matters when a low-sugar drink needs to remain thin.

In an original study of directly acidified protein drinks, Liu and colleagues used an experimental [CP Kelco HM pectin reported in the study](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7723220/fullTextXML) with DE 69%. The pectin-only formulations contained 0.20% and 0.40% w/w pectin.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-13">[13]</a> All samples were adjusted to pH 4.0, homogenised at 180–200 bar, heated at 121°C for 4 seconds and cooled. Instrumental and sensory measurements took place within one week.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-13">[13]</a>

The authors identified pectin-only and CMC A formulations as the lowest-viscosity systems in their comparison. They also reported smaller particle sizes at higher stabiliser concentrations in pectin-added samples, while describing particle-size changes across all systems as minor.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-13">[13]</a>

This study supports a protein-drink application under the reported conditions. It did not test calcium fortification, and the materials description does not identify the experimental pectin's botanical source.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-13">[13]</a> A commercial citrus grade, added-calcium formulation or longer shelf life would require its own evaluation. The reported heat treatment is an experimental condition, not a validated safety process for another drink.

For calcium-enriched drinks, H&F's [protein-stabilisation guide](https://www.herbstreith-fox.de/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/AWT_Ue_Acidified_milk_products_protein_stabilisation_en.pdf) identifies an additional consideration. Blockwise free carboxyl groups can make HM citrus pectins calcium-reactive, with increased solution viscosity after calcium addition. The guide recommends adding pectin and calcium separately and notes that less calcium-reactive apple pectins can be advantageous in this application.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-12">[12]</a>

A thin protein drink therefore needs a grade evaluated with its actual calcium salt, protein system and heat treatment. For a protein-free fortified juice, the selection would need evidence for that formulation rather than casein protection.

## A sample brief that supports a useful comparison

For a reduced-solids fruit spread, a sample request could read:

> Please propose non-amidated LM and, where suitable, amidated LM citrus pectins for the attached final pH, soluble-solids level and calcium system. The product must flow through filling and develop the specified spreadable texture on cooling, with acceptable particle formation and water release. Please provide the full grade name, DE and DA ranges, standardisation ingredients, and the application method used to characterise calcium reactivity and gel performance.

Attach the fruit base, calcium salt, process temperatures, residence times and cooling history. Request results on a clearly stated supplied-powder or pectin-content basis so that the samples can be compared consistently.

For an acidified protein drink, specify protein content, calcium addition, acidification route, homogenisation, heat treatment and drinking viscosity. H&F identifies these as relevant formulation and process variables, and assesses stability using viscosity, sediment after defined centrifugation and microscopic examination of protein particles.<a href="#citrus-pectin-gel-selection-ref-12">[12]</a>

The final selection should link the complete grade specification to observed performance in the intended process: filling and cooled texture for the spread, or particle stability and drinking viscosity for the protein drink.
