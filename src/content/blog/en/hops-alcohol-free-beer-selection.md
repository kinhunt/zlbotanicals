A beer can need more hop aroma without needing more bitterness. Fractionated hop oil, conventional isomerized extract and tetrahydro iso-alpha-acid extract address different parts of that formulation problem. Hopsteiner’s technical sheets illustrate the distinction: SELECTED FREE is positioned for aroma in alcohol-free and alcohol-reduced beer; Iso-Extract adjusts bitterness; Tetra supports a bittering system designed to resist lightstruck flavor.<a href="#hops-ref-5">[5]</a><a href="#hops-ref-7">[7]</a><a href="#hops-ref-6">[6]</a>

The practical choice starts with the beer’s missing attribute, rather than the highest extract concentration. Here, “alcohol-free” describes the application named by the sources, not a universal labeling threshold. The experiment discussed below tested beer at 0.5% ABV and above—not 0.0% beer or hop-flavored sparkling water.<a href="#hops-ref-3">[3]</a>

These are examples of material functions, not a supplier ranking. Their concentrations and sensory roles are not interchangeable.

## Aroma oil: composition and supplied dilution come first

Hopsteiner describes SELECTED FREE as conventionally extracted hop oil subsequently fractionated by distillation and diluted in propylene glycol. Its sheet characterizes the oil as mainly polar hop components and positions it specifically for alcohol-free and alcohol-reduced beer. The “not detectable” bittering-substance specification has no stated detection limit on the sheet.<a href="#hops-ref-5">[5]</a>

The recommended addition is on the cold side, typically before filtration. For addition before or during filtration, the supplier gives examples of up to 3 mL/hL for bottom-fermented beers and up to 6 mL/hL for top-fermented beers. These refer to the supplied product, not undiluted hop oil: they equal up to 0.03 and 0.06 mL/L respectively. The sheet proposes small additions to bottled beer with a microliter syringe to help establish a suitable amount.<a href="#hops-ref-5">[5]</a>

Those examples offer a starting point for evaluating this particular product. Pellets, neat oil and diluted oil cannot be substituted at the same mass or volume. For an aroma comparison, keep the beer base and bitterness adjustment consistent, then assess the aroma character delivered by each material under the intended addition process.

## What the alcohol-adjustment experiment actually shows

Cocuzza and colleagues examined a commercial wheat beer dealcoholized by thermal evaporation. They adjusted its ethanol content and dry-hopped it with Solero Type 90 pellets at 250 g/hL, equivalent to 2.5 g/L. Pellets were added loose to 20-litre NC kegs for semi-static contact over 14 days at 5°C.<a href="#hops-ref-3">[3]</a>

The dry-hopped groups were 0.5%, 3.5%, 7.0% and 10.5% ABV, with three samples per group. Each alcohol level had one unhopped control; the unadjusted base beer was 0.4% ABV. All 17 trial beers received Nagardo preservative. The published account is a 2023 BRAUWELT INTERNATIONAL excerpt of a 2022 model study, with supplier and TU Munich research-center authors.<a href="#hops-ref-3">[3]</a>

The original aroma table supports a compound-specific reading: linalool varies relatively little across the dry-hopped groups; β-myrcene is much lower at 0.5% than at the other levels; isobutyl isobutyrate rises between the first two groups and then changes little. Geraniol is nonmonotonic, with a lower mean at 10.5% than at 3.5% or 7.0% ABV.<a href="#hops-ref-3">[3]</a>

These are analytical concentrations, not aroma-intensity scores. The excerpt reports that most individual results were within or close to the analytical tolerance of ±10%, with exceptions; it does not supply the full individual results. The authors suggest sampling error for the geraniol dip, but that remains an explanation rather than a corrected measurement. They attribute exceptionally high myrcene concentrations to the closed system and very low yeast-cell content of the finished base beer.<a href="#hops-ref-3">[3]</a>

The useful finding is that reducing alcohol does not affect every hop constituent equally. It favors evaluating composition rather than assuming that all aroma delivery requires the same ethanol level. This pellet experiment did not test SELECTED FREE, and it does not establish that product’s performance in a finished beverage.

## Bittering extracts: mixing conditions matter

Iso-Extract is an aqueous solution of potassium salts of iso-alpha acids, specified at 30.0 ± 2.0% by mass by HPLC. It is intended for post-fermentation bitterness adjustment. Its sheet warns against prediluting the full-strength concentrate with beer because the lower pH causes precipitation, while recommending dosing into the beer stream where vigorous mixing is assured.<a href="#hops-ref-7">[7]</a>

Preparing a concentrated premix and adding a controlled dose to flowing beer are different operations. The supplier’s alkaline predilution instructions describe handling of the ingredient, not a target pH for the finished drink. If the base beer already has the desired bitterness, an aroma-led trial can leave this part of the formulation unchanged.

## Lightstruck protection is a whole-process choice

Tetra contains tetrahydro iso-alpha-acid potassium salts, distinct from conventional iso-alpha-acid salts. Hopsteiner makes protection against lightstruck flavor conditional on the absence of alpha acids and conventional iso-alpha acids. Its instructions cover all hop additions, equipment carryover and yeast previously exposed to conventional bittering materials.<a href="#hops-ref-6">[6]</a>

Adding Tetra alongside an existing conventional bittering system therefore does not automatically make the beer light-stable. Aroma retention is another endpoint: SELECTED FREE’s sheet states that its aroma components remain stable during beer aging, but supplies no finished-beer aging duration, light-exposure conditions or packaging comparison.<a href="#hops-ref-5">[5]</a>

For development, separate the aroma comparison from bitterness adjustment, then evaluate the selected combination in the intended package and storage conditions. A suitable aroma material, a controlled bittering addition and a lightstruck-resistant process answer three related—but different—questions.
