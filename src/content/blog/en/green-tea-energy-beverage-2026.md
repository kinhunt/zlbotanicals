## Start with the beverage, not the highest assay

Write down whether the brief needs tea flavor, a clear appearance or a defined catechin composition. A highly concentrated powder may bring astringency or haze that a lower-assay flavor-focused material does not. A green tea extract is not a substitute for validating the finished beverage.

## Compare like-for-like analytical data

Ask for separate EGCG, total catechins, caffeine and total polyphenol methods. Record dry-basis versus as-is reporting and carrier content. Do not compare a colorimetric total with an HPLC marker result as though they describe the same thing.

## Design a useful pilot

Compare candidate lots in the actual acid and water system, with and without the proposed sweetener. Measure clarity, sediment, color, astringency and caffeine; track catechin retention after heat processing and storage. Include oxygen, minerals, light and packaging. If ginseng is proposed, evaluate its identity and bitterness independently before combining.

## Keep the label separate from the ingredient assay

An EGCG value is not evidence of sustained energy. Have a qualified reviewer assess concentrated tea extract eligibility, caffeine exposure, intended users and warnings. Base nutrition and any claims on the complete finished product, not a supplier description.

## Questions to include in your RFQ

Send destination market, intended format, processing conditions, ingredient exclusions and sample needs. Ask for a current specification, batch-specific COA, full composition and risk-based contaminant testing. Specifications and availability are indicative until confirmed in writing; agree acceptance criteria and change-control terms before purchase.
