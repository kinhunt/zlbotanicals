A psyllium sample can help a gluten-free dough retain water and produce a softer crumb without making the finished bread lighter for its size. That is not necessarily a failed ingredient. It may mean that the bakery and supplier are using different definitions of improvement.

Three original bread studies show why the decision cannot rest on “high water binding” or “larger volume” alone. One found higher specific volume and lower crumb firmness with psyllium and adjusted water. Another found increased bread volume per flour input but <strong>lower volume per gram of finished bread</strong>. A third found that increasing psyllium raised instrumental crumb gumminess even after water was adjusted.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#psyllium-ref-2">[2]</a><a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

For a bakery choosing a husk powder, the useful question is therefore: <strong>which combination of material, water addition and product endpoint is being compared?</strong>

## First establish what the powder actually is

Psyllium husk powder and milled whole seeds are not interchangeable descriptions. In the 2022 rice-bread study, the researchers compared *Plantago psyllium* seeds, *Plantago ovata* seeds and *P. ovata* husk. All three materials were laboratory-milled to particles smaller than 300 µm, yet they produced different water-absorption and dough responses.<a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

At 9% replacement of rice flour, the reported Mixolab hydration values were 74.9% for *P. psyllium* seeds, 75.8% for *P. ovata* seeds and 98.1% for *P. ovata* husk, against 61.2% for the rice-flour control. These are the study's formulation/mixing measurements at a target torque of 1.1 ± 0.05 N·m—not a universal water-to-psyllium mixing ratio.<a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

The practical distinction is more specific than “fine versus coarse”: identify the species and plant fraction before comparing the particle-size specification. This study does not establish an optimum milling size; its materials shared an upper particle-size boundary, not a controlled particle-size distribution. A supplier's mesh designation alone cannot make a whole-seed result evidence for a husk powder.

## The strongest positive example changed water as well as psyllium

The 2021 study used a named milled husk material, VITACEL Psyllium P95, described by the authors as having 95% purity, an average particle size of 250 µm and approximately 80% dietary fibre. The purity and dietary-fibre figures are different descriptors; neither is a specification established for every commercial psyllium powder.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a>

Its bread base was 75 parts rice flour and 25 parts cassava starch. Eggs, milk powder, oil and other ingredients were also present. The trial therefore does not demonstrate performance in a vegan formula or in a simple flour–water system.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a>

The six firmness measurements came from two central slices from each of three loaves, not six independent baking batches. Added water is not total water including that supplied by other ingredients. Percentages in this study use a flour-weight basis, not finished-dough weight.

The three psyllium breads had significantly greater specific volume than the gluten-free control, but <strong>did not differ significantly from one another in specific volume</strong>. The highest-psyllium formulation had the lowest firmness among the gluten-free samples. The study thus supports a useful softness benefit without showing that progressively more psyllium produced progressively more loaf volume.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a>

There is another important feature in the formulation table: the low-psyllium bread used less added water than the control. These are selected psyllium–water combinations, not a single-variable psyllium dose series. Handling also differed: the control and lower-psyllium doughs were scraped into tins, while the intermediate and higher-psyllium doughs were moulded before panning.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a> Copying the 17.14% number while leaving the water and rest of the recipe unchanged is not a transfer of the tested formulation.

## Why a “larger loaf” claim can still mean denser bread

The 2022 study used a different system: rice flour partly replaced with seed or husk material, with water determined by Mixolab measurements. It reported that *P. ovata* husk increased bread volume expressed per 100 g of flour blend. Yet the paper also reported a decrease in <strong>specific volume</strong>, expressed as millilitres per gram of bread.<a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

Both observations can be true. The first denominator is the flour input; the second is the baked loaf mass. The authors linked the lower specific volume to higher retained water and heavier breads. A larger output volume from the same flour charge does not automatically mean a more aerated, lighter finished loaf.<a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

This distinction changes the commercial conversation. A bakery concerned with flour-to-product yield may value water retention, while one selling a light sandwich loaf may prioritise specific volume and eating texture. Neither endpoint should quietly stand in for the other.

The sensory findings also prevent an uncomplicated success story. The study's ten student panelists gave generally low acceptance ratings, and the authors identified a need to optimise the recipes, particularly those with husk. Fresh-bread scores did <strong>not</strong> differ significantly, so the lower numerical ratings should not be presented as a proven fresh-bread preference ranking. Softer instrumental texture was not, by itself, proof of a more liked product.<a href="#psyllium-ref-3">[3]</a>

## Water adjustment does not make “more psyllium” automatically better

The 2024 study began with a formulation already containing psyllium, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and xanthan gum. Its 100 g flour/starch base comprised 91.2 g maize starch, 5.5 g rice flour and 3.3 g tapioca starch, with a further 17.9 g commercial gluten-free bread mix and other ingredients added.<a href="#psyllium-ref-2">[2]</a> In its higher-psyllium version, psyllium increased from 9.8 to 13.2 g per 100 g flour and starch, while added water increased from 159.7 to 173.6 g. HPMC and xanthan remained at 3.6 and 3.2 g respectively. Water was adjusted through a subjective visual assessment intended to achieve similar dough consistencies.<a href="#psyllium-ref-2">[2]</a>

The higher-psyllium bread had a crumb gumminess of 40.7 ± 7.5 N versus 35.3 ± 4.9 N in the control, a statistically significant difference in the paper's Table 6. Crumb hardness, however, did not differ significantly between these two formulations. Here, *gumminess* is an instrumental texture-profile parameter, calculated from hardness and cohesiveness; it is not a consumer liking score.<a href="#psyllium-ref-2">[2]</a>

This result does not negate the 2021 softness finding. It shows that the starting recipe matters: a blend already structured with several hydrocolloids is not the same test as adding psyllium to a different gluten-free base. Nor is the study's water adjustment a generally validated addition rule. It was part of that recipe-development method.

## A softness result is not a preservative-free shelf-life claim

The 2021 paper followed breads for 72 hours and reported delayed firming and acceptable sensory results for the psyllium formulations. But its formula included calcium propionate, and the loaves were sprayed with mold inhibitors before polypropylene-bag storage at 22–25°C and 50–70% relative humidity.<a href="#psyllium-ref-1">[1]</a>

That evidence can support a discussion of texture retention under the studied conditions. It cannot establish that psyllium itself preserves a clean-label loaf, replaces mold control or guarantees a commercial shelf life. Those would require different evidence.

## Evaluate replacement and reformulation separately

For procurement, these papers suggest two separate questions rather than a hunt for the highest fibre or viscosity number. <strong>Can the candidate replace the current material at the current process settings? And what bread quality can it achieve after the recipe is adjusted?</strong> Treat these as two different development trials.

The first comparison keeps the existing formula and processing conditions fixed and reveals the disruption caused by a material change. The second allows water and, where necessary, processing to be adjusted, and tests the candidate as a formulation option. Do not combine the results as though both were a drop-in replacement test.

In either case, report the flour/starch basis, actual psyllium material, added water and other water-bearing ingredients alongside finished loaf mass, loaf volume, specific volume and crumb texture. Include the production-relevant handling endpoint—such as depositing or shaping—and a sensory assessment appropriate to the intended bread. The research above gives reasons to keep those outcomes separate, not universal pass limits.

The best candidate is not necessarily the one with the highest water binding. It is the material–water–process combination that meets the intended balance of handling, loaf yield, specific volume, softness and eating quality.
