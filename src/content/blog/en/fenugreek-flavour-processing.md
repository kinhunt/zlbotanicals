A fenugreek sample can smell appealing and still taste wrong in the finished food. For development work, “less strong” is therefore an incomplete target. Does it mean a quieter herbal aroma, less bitterness on eating, or simply better overall liking? Two primary studies illustrate why those questions should stay separate: one compared the aromas of commercial tinctures; the other measured liking of cookies containing processed fenugreek flour.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

The practical distinction is not just laboratory terminology. If the complaint is an intrusive smell, changing the aroma profile may be useful. If the complaint is bitterness, an aroma result alone does not establish that the problem has been solved. Begin with the sensory failure in the intended product, then choose the material and processing trial around it.

## A tincture is not a neutral version of seed flour

A 2025 study examined fenugreek tinctures from eight manufacturers, supplied through the Zhengzhou Tobacco Research Institute. Seven experienced assessors described aroma intensity, while GC–IMS and SPME–GC–MS were used to compare volatile constituents.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a> This is useful evidence of variation among extracts, not a demonstration that the tested materials were food-grade or suitable for a particular beverage.

The samples did not have one interchangeable “fenugreek smell.” HL-1, HL-2, HL-3 and HL-5 shared broadly burnt, herbal, hay, sweet and creamy characteristics. HL-4 was more prominently spicy, with strong sweet and herbal notes, while HL-6 included an ointment-like character alongside burnt, herbal and hay notes.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a> For a flavour-development team, that is a reason to describe the desired character rather than request merely a “mild” extract.

A sweet-smelling sample is not necessarily sweet-tasting. The panel in this paper scored aroma attributes; it did not produce a bitterness-intensity curve or a consumer preference ranking.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a> In a purchasing brief, “sweet aroma” should therefore remain an aroma description, not become a promise of a sweet or non-bitter finished product.

The analytical work also needs a proportionate reading. The paper used relative quantification with a correction factor of one for each analyte, and calculated odour activity values using thresholds from aqueous solutions.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a> Those results help interpret the comparison, but they are not ready-made acceptance limits for every food matrix. A useful next step is to take candidate materials into the actual recipe, rather than select one solely because a volatile peak or calculated odour activity value is lower.

## Soaking and germination do not establish a bitterness-free flour

The food-processing evidence is especially instructive. In a 2020 cookie study, fenugreek seeds were soaked for 24 hours at about 20°C in a 1:5 seed-to-water ratio, expressed as weight/volume. The water was discarded, the seeds were rinsed, then germinated for 72 hours at about 20°C with frequent watering. They were oven-dried at 60°C for 12 hours and ground to pass a 710 μm sieve.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

That is a defined processed seed flour, not an extract and not untreated ground seed. It is also a multi-step treatment. The study did not compare raw and germinated fenugreek at the same inclusion level, so it cannot quantify how much bitterness germination removed.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

The researchers replaced portions of wheat flour with both fenugreek and oat flour. Their three blends contained 5% fenugreek with 10% oat, 10% with 20%, and 15% with 30%; the remaining flour was wheat.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a> These are percentages of the flour blend, not percentages of the complete cookie. The recipe also contained sugar, sunflower oil, water, vanilla extract, baking powder and salt, so the findings belong to a sweet baked-food system.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

Ten trained panelists used a nine-point hedonic scale. The important word is *hedonic*: this measured liking, not the intensity of bitterness.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a> The distinction matters when reading the results:

These are the blend means in Table 5, not separate results measured only at 175°C. The two higher-fenugreek blends share the same statistical group for taste; their numerical difference does not demonstrate a further significant fall between them.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

The authors suggested that fenugreek bitterness could explain the lower taste scores. They also acknowledged that scores of 3.7 and 3.4 could indicate that consumers would not like those blends.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a> However, fenugreek and oat increased together while wheat decreased. The experiment therefore does not isolate fenugreek dose as the only cause, and its small trained panel is not a broad consumer acceptance study.

There is a useful tension within the paper: the abstract and conclusion recommend the 10% fenugreek/20% oat blend baked at 175°C as nutritionally rich with acceptable sensory attributes, but the detailed discussion flags the poor taste scores.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a> For product development, keep the taste result visible rather than reducing the paper to “10% fenugreek is acceptable.” Germination was part of the preparation; it did not make acceptance automatic.

## Baking hotter was not a demonstrated flavour fix

The cookies were baked for 10 minutes at 150, 175 or 200°C.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a> In the reported analysis, baking temperature did not significantly affect odour liking, taste liking or overall acceptability; their temperature-effect p-values were 0.906, 0.471 and 0.109 respectively. Colour and appearance, by contrast, scored less favourably at 200°C.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

This does not prove that heating can never change fenugreek flavour. It says something narrower and more useful: in this recipe and temperature range, simply baking hotter was not shown to improve those sensory outcomes. Nor does cookie baking establish the performance of a separate seed-roasting or extract-deodorisation process.

## Turn “low bitterness” into a meaningful sample comparison

A good brief starts with the material. Keep whole-seed flour, processed flour and a liquid extract as separate candidates; do not substitute one for another on the strength of the botanical name. Ask what was actually done to the seed or extract, and what basis is used for any dilution or addition rate. The two studies above examined different materials and different sensory endpoints, so they cannot supply a universal conversion between them.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>

For a bakery project, an informative first comparison would hold the oat proportion, sugar, flavouring and baking schedule constant while changing only the fenugreek candidate or its dose. This is a proposed development design, not a procedure validated by the cited cookie study. It directly addresses the study's coupled ingredient changes.

For a drink or another untested application, start afresh in that matrix. Assess the aroma before tasting, then record bitterness during consumption and the lingering aftertaste separately from overall liking. Include a product-base control and coded candidate samples. If a process is being sold as “debittering,” compare its before-and-after materials under the same conditions rather than accepting a pleasant aroma as the endpoint.

The buying decision then becomes more specific: which material delivers the desired flavour at the intended use level after the actual process? The available primary evidence supports asking that question carefully. It does not support a universal claim that germination removes bitterness, that higher baking temperature fixes flavour, or that an aroma-screened tincture will be acceptable in food.<a href="#fenugreek-ref-1">[1]</a><a href="#fenugreek-ref-2">[2]</a>
